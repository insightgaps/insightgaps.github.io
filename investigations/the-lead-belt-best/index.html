The story in scenes

Scene 1 · Claims R-02, R-03

The walk is 59 metres long

Every school morning, a child walks through a school gate in Rosulpur, Kamrangir Char, in Dhaka. Fifty-nine metres away stands a battery recycling factory. That is not a metaphor for closeness. It is a measured distance, gate to factory, and the soil between the two holds lead at 330,001 parts per million. Lead does not biodegrade. It does not wash away with the next rain, and it does not become harmless because a school year ends.

Hold that walk in your head before any other number arrives, because every other number in this story is a way of asking the same question at a larger scale: if the furnace is this close to one school gate, how many gates is it close to? Eighteen schools sit within 500 metres of this single furnace alone. The furnace was not targeted in the April 2025 enforcement drive that closed sites elsewhere. It was still there, beside the gate, while the drive went on around it.

Decision point 1 — decide first: is 59 metres a shocking distance, or an ordinary one in a crowded city?

Reveal — Claims R-02, R-03 · Confirmed. The 59 m gate-to-factory distance, the 330,001 ppm soil reading, and the 825× multiple of the 400 ppm residential threshold are the published figures for BD-4591, and 18 schools within 500 metres of this furnace is the published single-furnace count. Crowding does not change the reading: the threshold is a safety threshold, not a crowding allowance, and 825 times that threshold in soil beside a school gate is the finding, whatever you call the distance.

Scene 2 · Claim R-03

The highest reading is 1,702 times safe

Now widen the lens from one gate to the worst soil in the report. At Kathgora, in Savar, the peak reading reaches 680,872 parts per million. Divide that by the 400 ppm residential safety threshold and you get 1,702, rounded down from 1,702.18. That is not a percentage over the limit, or a small breach, or a rounding error. It is soil holding more than seventeen hundred times the amount considered safe for a home.

But look at the third bar before you memorise the ranking. Jatrabari's 744× is drawn as solidly as the other two, and it is not backed the same way: no ppm value for BD-4802 is published in the live summary. A multiple without its measurement is a claim you cannot re-check, and this story will not pretend otherwise. The honest ranking is this: two bars you can verify from their parts per million, and one bar you must take on the report's word until its ppm value is published.

Decision point 2 — decide first: would you publish the 744× bar next to the other two, or hold it back until its ppm value appears?

Reveal — Claim R-03 · Confirmed. The peak reading and its multiple are confirmed: 680,872 ppm at Kathgora, Savar (BD-4921), about 1,702 times the 400 ppm threshold. The Jatrabari 744× figure is published in the live report, but its underlying ppm value is not published, so it is flagged here, not smoothed over. Publishing it with the flag is the bureau's choice; holding it back would also have been defensible, and the reader should know the difference.

Scene 3 · Claims R-01, R-02

294 sites, and the difference between 145 and 121

Step back again, now to the whole country. Pure Earth and the University of Dhaka assessed 294 contaminated sites across Bangladesh, in 93 upazilas and every division. Of those, 44 are the formally assessed headline sites with schools nearby. Around those 44 sites, the analysis counts 145 school–site intersections within 500 metres. That sentence is easy to misread, and the misreading matters: 145 is not 145 schools.

A single school can sit within 500 metres of more than one site, and each pairing counts as an intersection. Twenty-four such overlaps sit inside the 145, which is why the unique-school count is 121. If you walk away remembering "145 schools", you have overstated the school count by 24 and understated the way the danger overlaps — the same school, in range of two furnaces instead of one. The report's original dek made exactly that mistake, and correction work in September 2026 fixed it to intersections and unique schools. This draft keeps the fixed wording.

Decision point 3 — decide first: if a headline said "145 schools", would you correct it, and to what?

Reveal — Claims R-01, R-02 · Confirmed. The published counts are 294 sites, 44 headline sites, 145 intersections, and 121 unique schools. The correction is to "145 school–site intersections (121 unique schools) within 500 metres" — both numbers, every time, never the first number alone as a school count.

Scene 4 · Claim R-02 — the count that moves

The map moved, so the count moved

Here is where the story stops being tidy. Re-run the same analysis against an archived snapshot of OpenStreetMap schools, across all 294 sites rather than the 44 headline sites, and the numbers change: 51 sites with schools, 166 intersections, 125 unique schools. Count again on the methodology page, including schools newly mapped in OpenStreetMap, and you get 166 intersections with 132 unique schools. The same 166 intersections now produce two different unique-school counts, 125 and 132, and no derivation for the gap between them is published.

The report's stated cause is OpenStreetMap database drift: the map changed between the original extraction window and the archived snapshot, so the count changed with it. That is a plausible cause and it is not a proof; drift explains why counts differ, but it does not reconcile 125 with 132. This story therefore does what the published report does: it keeps the published figures as the publication of record, shows the other counts beside them, and refuses to pick a winner silently. A count built on a moving map is a count with a moving answer, and any retelling that quotes only one number is hiding that.

Decision point 4 — decide first: which school count would you put in a headline — 121, 125, or 132?

Reveal — Claim R-02 · Confirmed, with the discrepancy flagged. The headline count is 121 unique schools, because it belongs to the published 145-intersection analysis of the 44 headline sites. The 125 and 132 figures belong to different snapshots and counting rules, and the 125-versus-132 gap has no published derivation. Quoting 132 as if it were the same analysis as 121 would be a category error, not an update.

Scene 5 · Claim R-06

Where a child lives decides the odds

Soil beside a gate is one kind of evidence. Blood is another. In the UNICEF and Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics MICS survey of 2025, 38.3% of children nationally exceed the safe blood-lead limit — and in Dhaka Division the figure is 65.2%, against 15.9% in Rajshahi. That is a gap of 49.3 percentage points between two divisions of the same country, and it means a child's postcode, in effect, decides whether they are more likely than not to be over the limit.

Guard the definition here, because it is easy to break. These are population survey rates. They are not the clinical cohort figure that sometimes travels with this story: 98% of 500 tested Dhaka toddlers aged two to four exceeded the reference standard in one clinical study. That cohort was tested because it was at risk; quoting 98% as a Dhaka or national rate would turn a clinic's waiting room into a country. The survey says 65.2% in Dhaka. The clinic says something narrower, and scarier, about a smaller group. Both are true, and neither is the other.

Decision point 5 — decide first: a colleague quotes "98% of Dhaka children". Do you let it stand?

Reveal — Claim R-06 · Confirmed. The confirmed divisional figures are MICS 2025 survey rates: national 38.3%, Dhaka 65.2%, Rajshahi lowest at 15.9%. The 98% figure is a clinical cohort (500 Dhaka toddlers aged 2–4, Sultana et al., 2026) and must never be quoted as a population rate. Correct the colleague, with the cohort label attached.

Scene 6 · Claim R-07

Six against six thousand

Why is the furnace beside the gate there at all? Follow the battery. Bangladesh has six formal, licensed recyclers, handling about 30% of national battery waste. Against them stand more than 6,000 informal, unregistered workshops, handling about 70% — without emission scrubbers. That is a thousand informal workshops for every formal one, producing more than twice the waste share of the licensed sector. The furnace beside the school gate is not an accident of planning. It is the normal output of a chain in which the regulated part is the minority.

Enforcement, when it comes, comes in bursts. In April 2025 the Department of Environment shut down 28 facilities in Jatrabari, and the drive reached Savar too. A shutdown is an event, and it matters — but it is not the same as the chain changing. The workshops are numerous, small, and replaceable; the licensed capacity that could absorb their waste is six firms. Until that ratio moves, every closure is a gap that the informal chain has every incentive to fill again.

Decision point 6 — decide first: after 28 closures, is the chain fixed, dented, or unchanged?

Reveal — Claim R-07 · Confirmed. Six formal recyclers at about 30% against 6,000+ informal workshops at about 70% without scrubbers is the published chain. Twenty-eight closures dent a chain of six thousand; they do not remake it. The answer is "dented", and anyone claiming "fixed" has confused an enforcement event with a structural change.

Scene 7 · Claim R-05

39,875 — and the sentence you must never write

Multiply the 145 intersections by an average school enrolment of 275 pupils, taken from the Annual Primary School Census of 2024, and you get about 39,875. Counted by unique schools instead — 121 schools times 275 — the estimate is about 33,275, and the audited range around the projection runs from 24,650 to 50,750. Those are the numbers, and they are useful, because they turn an abstract map of intersections into a sense of how many school places sit inside the buffer.

But they are not children. No child was tested to produce 39,875; no blood sample sits behind it. It is a projection of exposure instances: school places, multiplied by pairings, under an average that no individual school is obliged to match. The sentence you must never write is "39,875 children poisoned". The earlier draft of this story's live report did write something close to that — "children at extreme risk" — and a September 2026 correction reframed it exactly as a projection. The correction is part of the story now, because it shows the bureau catching its own overstatement in public.

Decision point 7 — decide first: a headline offers "39,875 children poisoned". Do you run it?

Reveal — Claim R-05 · Supported. The projection is supported, not confirmed, precisely because it rests on an average enrolment model rather than counted, tested children. The headline must read "about 39,875 exposure instances projected", with the unique-school estimate (about 33,275) and the audited range (24,650–50,750) available beside it. Running "children poisoned" would turn a model into a body count.

Scene 8 · Claim R-08

Sixty-eight critical zones, sixty-five over the line

Not every contaminated site is equally dangerous, and the report draws a line inside its own map. Sixty-eight sites are designated critical zones. Of those, 65 exceed 100,000 parts per million — two hundred and fifty times the safe threshold. The three-site gap between 68 and 65 is not a rounding error to be tidied away; it is the difference between a designation and a measurement, and merging them would invent three sites over the line that the data does not place there.

Think about what 100,000 ppm means beside the earlier numbers. The gate at Kamrangir Char sits at 330,001 ppm, more than three times this critical line. Kathgora's peak is nearly seven times it. The critical line is not the worst case; it is the floor of the worst category, and 65 sites clear it. That is the scale of the most dangerous ground in the country, stated without exaggeration and without needing any.

Decision point 8 — decide first: would you write "68 sites over 100,000 ppm" for speed?

Reveal — Claim R-08 · Confirmed. The confirmed figures are 68 designated critical zones, with 65 exceeding 100,000 ppm (250× safe). Writing "68 over 100,000 ppm" would be wrong by three sites and would erase the designation-versus-measurement distinction that correction C-005 was written to protect.

Scene 9 · Claim R-04

Twenty-two years, zero cleanups

This is the longest scene in the story, and it is told in dates. In 2003, the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics documented 12,207 battery recycling operations — the problem, counted, more than two decades ago. In 2019, Pure Earth and the University of Dhaka assessed more than 270 sites and mapped them, and no permanent closures followed publication. In February 2025 came the first formal memorandum of understanding in 22 years. In April 2025 came the closures: 28 sites in Jatrabari and Savar, while BD-4591, the gate in Scene 1, was untouched.

And at the end of that timeline, the public record holds zero permanent remediations at the analysed coordinates. Not few. Zero. Enforcement closures and demolitions happened, in Savar, Narsingdi, and Mymensingh as well as Jatrabari — and none of them is a remediation, because none of them is recorded as contaminated soil removed, treated, and verified clean. The timeline is the story's indictment precisely because it is so undramatic: counted, mapped, promised, closed in places — and the soil, as far as the public record shows, still there.

Decision point 9 — decide first: a demolished smelter — do you call the site cleaned up?

Reveal — Claim R-04 · Confirmed. Zero contaminated sites have been permanently remediated or cleaned up, on the public record at the coordinates analysed. A demolition removes a building; remediation removes and verifies the contamination in the soil. Until a public record shows the second, the answer is no — and the scope matters: absence of a record is not proof that no remediation ever happened anywhere, only that none is on the public record at these coordinates.

Scene 10 · Satellite — corroboration, never proof

The view from above cannot taste the soil

There is a photograph-shaped hole in this story, and it sits here deliberately. Satellite verification in March 2026 reported visual indicators of continued activity at BD-4802 in Jatrabari, and the live report described that activity as continuing "351 days after the crackdown". That duration cannot be checked: the crackdown date is given only as a month, April 2025, and a day-count needs a day. Without a starting day, "351 days" is arithmetic performed on a date that was never that precise, and this story flags it rather than repeating it as a measured interval.

More importantly, a satellite image cannot measure what this story is about. It can show a furnace beside school grounds, and it can show change over time against a baseline. It cannot show parts per million in soil, or micrograms in blood, or emissions in air. That is why the wording rule exists and why it is repeated at every satellite mention: corroboration, never proof, always paired with ground or laboratory data. The satellite pairs for BD-4591 and BD-4802 exist in the live repository, but their sensor, date, resolution, cloud cover, and baseline are unstated in this draft — so no pair is reproduced here. A labelled gap, in the step where the claim is made, is more honest than a handsome image with no provenance.

Decision point 10 — decide first: a striking satellite image arrives with no date, sensor, or baseline. Do you publish it?

Reveal — satellite rule, applied throughout. No. An undated, unbased image can show that something is near something else, and nothing more. Publish it only once sensor, date, resolution, cloud cover, and baseline are verified per pair, captioned as corroboration with the ground reading it corroborates — never as proof of operation, and never as a measurement of lead.

Scene 11 · The verdict is yours

The gate is still 59 metres away

Return to where the story started, because the story has not moved the gate. The child still walks 59 metres from a school gate to a furnace. The soil still reads 330,001 ppm beside that walk. The country still holds 294 mapped sites, 145 intersections, 121 unique schools in the published count — and a second and third count that do not agree with the first, because the map they were counted from moved. The chain still runs six against six thousand. The modelled toll still hangs over the whole picture, labelled as modelled, never as counted: 138,054 cardiovascular deaths a year and $28.63 billion, about 6% of GDP, from Lancet Planetary Health in 2023 — a model of what lead costs, not a register of the dead.

So here is the judgment the narrator will not make for you. The published proposals are clear enough: publish the full certificate registry; inspect or serve closure orders on all 44 school-adjacent sites within 90 days; commission a national soil remediation programme within one year, prioritising the 18 schools near BD-4591. Those are proposals, not findings, and they belong to the live report, not to this narrator. What the findings support is narrower and harder: after 22 years of counting and mapping, the public record shows zero permanent cleanups, and the first thing that would have to change before that sentence changes is not another closure. It is a record, published, of soil cleaned and verified clean at a named coordinate. Until that record exists, the gate stays 59 metres away — and the story stays unfinished.

Decision point 11 — decide first: what single record would change your verdict on this story?

Reveal — the test. A published remediation record at a named analysed coordinate: soil removed or treated, verified clean by testing, on the public record. Not a closure order, not a demolition photograph, not a satellite image, not a promise. That record, and only that record, moves the zero. Everything else in this story — the gate, the multiples, the counts, the chain — is already on the record, waiting for it.

What remains flagged (short list — full detail in the appendix)

  • School counts disagree: published 145 / 44 / 121 vs re-run 51 / 166 / 125 vs methodology 132 unique schools on the same 166 intersections (no derivation for 125 vs 132). Published figures stay the record; the rest are shown, not merged.
  • BD-4802 744× has no published ppm value. Its multiple cannot be re-checked from a measurement in this draft.
  • Satellite "351 days" is unverifiable from a month-only April 2025 crackdown date; sensor, date, resolution, cloud, and baseline are unstated per pair, so no satellite pair is reproduced here.
  • Master dataset v5 is not in the repository, so per-site values cannot be independently re-run from this draft alone; the replication script is referenced, not republished.
  • 39,875 is a projection of exposure instances (145 × 275), never "children poisoned"; unique-school estimate about 33,275; audited range 24,650–50,750.
  • Historical blood-lead figures are not current figures: a 2000 Dhaka study (779 children, mean 15.0 µg/dL) belongs to the leaded-petrol era and must never be presented as current.
  • Absence of a remediation record is not proof that no remediation ever occurred anywhere — it is a public-record finding at analysed coordinates.

What to watch next

  • Whether any permanent remediation record appears at the analysed coordinates — closures and demolitions are not remediation.
  • Whether the Department of Environment publishes the full Environmental Clearance Certificate registry, and whether the 44 school-adjacent sites are inspected or served closure orders within the proposed 90 days.
  • Whether a national soil remediation programme is commissioned, prioritising the 18 schools within 500 metres of BD-4591.
  • Whether the master dataset v5 is restored to the repository, so per-site values can be independently re-run.
  • Whether the BD-4802 ppm value and the satellite pairs' metadata (sensor, date, resolution, cloud, baseline) are published, so the flagged claims can be checked.
