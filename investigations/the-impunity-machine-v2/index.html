Step 1 · The rates

Do not pick one rate. Each is correct on its own denominator — and wrong if compared as if denominators were the same.

Step 1 · Claim R-01 · Confirmed

Imagine the waiting room first, before any chart. Over twenty-three years, 66,711 women came through One-Stop Crisis Centres. At the end of that long corridor, the published record counts 310 convictions: 0.46% — 310 divided by 66,711 — on the denominator of visitors, not of cases filed or decided. Hold that picture: everything that follows is about why so few arrivals ever become a conviction, and why the number you quote depends on where you stand in the corridor.

There is no official conviction rate for this law. Bangladesh has never published one. What exists instead is a shelf of independent measurements, each honest on its own terms and each measuring something slightly different: 0.12% of filed cases (Police Headquarters, 2012–2017); 0.39% of filed cases (Naripokkho, six districts, 2011–2018); the 0.46% of visitors you have just seen; 0.77% and 0.86% of disposed cases in two older studies; 2.0% of filed cases (UN Women, 2013–2019); and a 3.66% ceiling at accused level among disposed cases (BRAC/Agile, 2021–2022). The range is real, not measurement error to be averaged away — and averaging it would be the first lie this story refuses to tell.

Decision point 1 — decide first: which rate would you quote in a headline?

Reveal — Claim R-01 · Confirmed. Only 310 convictions from 66,711 centre visitors over 23 years: 0.46% on that denominator. Whichever rate you picked — 0.12%, 0.46%, 3.66% — print its denominator beside it, or you have misled your reader. The "twelve measurements" claim stays flagged above, not presented as fact.

Step 2 · The funnel

Stages, not rates. The widths are illustrative only — do not read them as percentages.

Step 2 · Claim R-01 · Confirmed

Now walk the corridor. The first door is disclosure, and most people never open it: 62–64% of women do not disclose non-partner sexual violence (UNFPA/BBS Violence Against Women Survey 2024, 27,476 women interviewed). That is not a failure of courage. It is the first narrowing, and it happens before any institution is involved.

After disclosure come police contact, a charge sheet, a trial, and finally a conviction — each stage losing most of what entered it. This story gives you no rate for each step, because the published record supplies no single clean set of stage rates, and inventing them would be worse than leaving them blank. What it does supply is the two ends of the journey: tens of thousands of arrivals at one end, 310 convictions at the other, and rates in Step 1 that all land below 4% on any denominator. A system that looks severe on paper produces almost no punishment in practice, and the funnel is where that severity leaks away.

Decision point 2 — decide first: where would you intervene first?

Reveal — Claim R-01 · Confirmed (funnel reading). Disclosure is the widest loss: 62–64% never disclose. Fixing only the trial cannot recover what was lost at the first door — which is why Step 10's proposals span the whole journey, not one door.

Step 3 · Severity

A timeline, not a trend chart. No bar height here represents a rate.

Step 3 · Claim R-02 · Confirmed

Halfway down the corridor is a sign promising the harshest punishment in the law. In October 2020 the Act was amended to add the death penalty for all rape. If severity were the problem, that should have been the turning point. The published sequence says it was not: the conviction rate did not improve, and the backlog grew.

It does not claim a controlled experiment — none exists, and the register says so plainly. It claims a sequence: the harshest penalty was added, reporting attrition, backlog, evidence loss, and witness risk were not solved, and the queue kept growing. Severity without certainty is a threat the system cannot carry out, and a threat it cannot carry out deters nobody. That is the machine's central irony, placed here, mid-journey, rather than as a footnote to a model.

Decision point 3 — decide first: would you call this system "tougher" after 2020?

Reveal — Claim R-02 · Confirmed. Maximum legal severity with near-zero certainty: the death-penalty expansion did not solve reporting attrition, backlog, evidence loss, or witness risk. That wording rests on the published sequence, not a controlled experiment — and the caveat stays attached.

Step 4 · Centres

Geography is access. Most districts have no full centre at all.

Step 4 · Claim R-03 · Confirmed

Suppose she discloses and sets out to find help. In 50 of 64 districts there is no full One-Stop Crisis Centre. Fourteen centres exist in total, concentrated in divisional headquarters — 14 is 64 minus 50, calculated, matching the live report's metadata. Geography here is not background. It is the next door, and for most of the country it is closed.

Take one division. Barishal has the highest intimate-partner violence rate nationally at 81.5%, in the same survey — and one centre for its entire division, about nine million people, in the divisional city, a bus journey away for most residents. No map is offered: no verified coordinates are published here, and a schematic must never pose as a map. You do not need one to feel the distance: one building, nine million people, a bus fare between them.

Decision point 4 — decide first: is 14 centres a national system?

Reveal — Claim R-03 · Confirmed. Infrastructure remains thin: 14 centres, two national DNA machines, major unprocessed backlogs, no complete national registry for key outcomes. Fourteen buildings are not a national system for 64 districts.

Step 5 · The window

The window closes before most arrivals. The 22% figure is derived (100 − 78) and labelled as such.

Step 5 · Claim R-07 · Confirmed

A clock runs from the moment of assault, faster than the journey to help. Forensic evidence is most usable within 72 hours, yet 78% of survivors arrive after that window (BRAC 2022). The chart's other side — 22% within it — is derived by subtraction (100 − 78) and labelled derived, because BRAC published the 78%, not the 22%.

Section 32 mandates hospital examination. But no institution tracks whether hospitals refuse, or how often, or where. So the law promises an examination, the clock punishes delay, and nobody counts the refusals in between. A survivor can do everything asked — disclose, travel, arrive — and still arrive too late for the evidence the trial will later demand, through no delay the system has measured.

Decision point 5 — decide first: whose delay is this?

Reveal — Claim R-07 · Confirmed. 78% arrive after the 72-hour window (BRAC 2022), while §32 mandates hospital examination — and no institution tracks refusal compliance. Blaming the survivor blames the person the clock was already running against. Untracked refusals are a confirmed absence (Step 10), not a measured zero.

Step 6 · DNA

The forensic bottleneck, in one bar.

Step 6 · Claim R-04 · Confirmed

If she arrives in time and an examination happens, the evidence still has to travel. DNA from regional centres must be processed at the central National Forensic DNA Profiling Laboratory before reaching a specialised tribunal. That link is stalled at scale: of 1,304 samples received in 2025, 1,204 remained unprocessed in September 2025 — 92% (1,204 ÷ 1,304 = 92.3%, published as 92%). The 100 processed, 8%, are derived (1,304 − 1,204) and labelled derived.

There are two national DNA machines for about 170 million people. Beside the first number sits a second that does not quite explain itself: 789 cases stalled in September 2025. This story does not equate the two. Stalled cases and unprocessed samples are different counts from the same bottleneck, and the record does not say one is a subset of the other. Hold both without merging them: the system built a queue at its narrowest point, then left it there.

Decision point 6 — decide first: would you merge 789 stalled cases into 1,204 unprocessed samples?

Reveal — Claim R-04 · Confirmed. DNA backlog 92%: 1,204 of 1,304 samples received in 2025 unprocessed at the National Forensic DNA Profiling Laboratory, September 2025. The 789 stalled cases are a separate count (GA-007); do not merge the two.

Step 7 · The wait

The limit versus the wait — 26.1 times longer. The 90-day compliance rate itself is unpublished.

Step 7 · Claim R-05 · Confirmed

Now the waiting begins, the longest part of the story. The average verdict wait is 2,349 days — over six years (BRAC 2022, 16 districts, 385 cases) — against a statutory tribunal limit of 90 days. That is 26.1 times the limit the law sets (2,349 ÷ 90).

The 90-day bar is a sliver beside the 2,349-day bar, and that sliver is the point: the law's promise is visible only because the failure beside it is so large. What the chart cannot show is whether the limit is ever met, because its compliance rate is unpublished — a confirmed absence, not a measured zero. A limit nobody measures is a limit nobody has to meet.

Decision point 7 — decide first: is a 90-day limit without a compliance rate a real limit?

Reveal — Claim R-05 · Confirmed. Average verdict wait 2,349 days, over six years, against a 90-day limit whose compliance rate is unpublished. An unpublished compliance rate is a finding here, not a footnote.

Before / after: the limit, then the wait

Before, the law as written: a verdict within 90 days. After, as measured: 2,349 days on average (BRAC 2022, 16 districts, 385 cases). The 2,259-day gap is where cases, witnesses, and evidence grow old.

Step 8 · The queue

The queue grows by design of throughput, not by one busy year.

Step 8 · Claim R-08 · Confirmed

While one case waits six years, the queue behind it grows. In 2024, 17,544 new cases under this Act were filed and 5,648 disposed of — 11,896 added to the backlog in one year, filings outpacing disposals by about 3.1 times (17,544 ÷ 5,648 = 3.11). This is not one busy year. It is arithmetic: taking in three cases for every one finished, the queue cannot shrink.

By March 2025 the pending pile stood at approximately 150,000 cases — 148,314 (SHARE-Net) and 151,317 (Daily Star), reported as approximately 150,000 because the two counts likely reflect different cutoff dates. Both figures stay visible rather than the tidier one being picked, because the difference is itself information: even the queue's size is counted twice, differently.

Decision point 8 — decide first: at 3.1 to 1, what happens next year?

Reveal — Claim R-08 · Confirmed. In 2024, filings (17,544) outpaced disposals (5,648) by about 3.1 times, adding 11,896 cases in one year. If the ratio holds, the queue — and the years-long wait — grows again.

Step 9 · The model

A model, not a count. Quote it only with its range — the ceiling is roughly 800 times the floor.

Step 9 · Claim R-06 · Supported

At the corridor's end, add the doors together: what is the chance, all told, that punishment follows? The published answer is a model, not a count. On the mid-reference model, Model A, the compound probability is about 0.0215% — roughly 1 in 4,650 — built as 0.0146 × 0.59 × 0.025. Model B gives 0.0400% (1 in 2,500); Model C, the ceiling, gives 0.2756% (1 in about 360). Across sensitivity variants the range runs from a floor of 0.00034% (1 in about 294,000) to that 0.2756% ceiling — under 0.3% on every variant, but spanning roughly 800-fold.

The model's direction is stable and its single number is not. It assumes stages are independent, when in reality they are conditional: a case that survives disclosure is not a random case, and neither is one that reaches trial. Survey inputs carry confidence intervals and self-report biases. So quote the model as a range, with its pathway — or not at all. A bare "0.0215%" without floor, ceiling, and the word "model" is a precision the evidence does not support.

Decision point 9 — decide first: would you publish 0.0215% on its own?

Reveal — Claim R-06 · Supported. Compound punishment probability about 0.0215% (1 in about 4,650) on the mid-reference model, range 0.00034% to 0.2756%. Supported, not Confirmed: the inputs are confirmed, the compound is modelled. Refusing to publish it bare is the discipline this claim requires.

Step 10 · The absences

Twelve datasets confirmed absent (GA-001 to GA-012). Absence here is an accountability finding, not a research limitation.

Step 10 · Twelve confirmed-absent datasets

And now the last door, which is not a door at all. Twelve datasets that should exist do not exist in publishable form: annual conviction rates by tribunal and district; FIR registration rates; 90-day compliance rates; national §17 prosecution counts; withdrawal and abandonment rates; pre- and post-2020 comparisons; DNA times by district and case type; the witness protection law, drafted and pending since 2015, still not enacted; the marital rape exemption, which CEDAW required removed in 2016, still in force in 2026; district-level conviction data; disposal-versus-conviction breakdowns; §32 hospital-refusal tracking.

Each absence is held or should exist, and none is published in the form needed — that is a confirmed absence here, after search across institutional channels, not a guess. Each blinds a different stage of the journey you have just walked: without tribunal rates you cannot see which courts fail; without FIR rates you cannot see which complaints vanish at the police station; without DNA times by district you cannot see which regions wait longest; without a witness protection law you cannot ask why witnesses go silent and expect an answer the law can protect.

So the story ends — not with a villain unmasked, but with what would have to exist for the machine to work differently. The published proposals are proposals, not findings, and stay labelled as such: 50 or more district-level centres; at least eight regional DNA labs with a 72-hour target; enact the Witness Protection Bill; quarterly publication of six key metrics; a §17 national registry; criminalising marital rape. Against them, set the twelve absences. The distance between those two lists is the distance between the machine as it is and as it was promised. You have walked the corridor, chosen a rate, refused the CEDAW trap, and refused to quote a model bare; judging that distance is now yours. That judgment — not a verdict handed down here — is the close.

Decision point 10 — decide first: what would you ask for first?

Would you ask first for tribunal conviction rates, so failing courts can be named, or for the witness protection law, so witnesses can speak safely? Either answer is defensible; claiming either question is already answered is not. No new allegation is made here beyond what is published, and no reply is recorded — a fresh, logged reply attempt is required before any new allegation.
