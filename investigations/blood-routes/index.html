Step 1 · The Rush

The 15-Day Surge: 351 Deaths

During the 15-day Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 travel period, 351 people were killed in road crashes across Bangladesh — an average of 23.4 fatalities per day. This headline figure reflects published monitoring by the Road Safety Foundation (RSF) and Bangladesh Jatri Kalyan Samity (BJKS). Because the underlying incident logs sit outside the bureau’s 2022–2024 cleaned dataset and are not yet deposited in the public archive, this number is audited as a reported finding rather than an independent recount.

Key Finding (Claim R-01 · Supported): 351 deaths across 15 travel days (23.4/day, rounded to 23), reported by RSF/BJKS monitoring.

Step 2 · Two Definitions

Two Ledgers: Police FIRs vs. 30-Day Mortality

Official road safety statistics in Bangladesh diverge sharply because two institutions count fundamentally different events. The Bangladesh Road Transport Authority (BRTA) counts only incidents registered in police First Information Reports (FIRs). This tally excludes victims who die en route to hospitals, families who reach informal out-of-court settlements, and post-admission hospital deaths.

Conversely, the World Health Organization (WHO) applies the international standard: all deaths occurring within 30 days of a collision, modelled from health facility surveys and trauma records. The gap between them is primarily an institutional definition gap.

Key Finding (Claim R-02 · Supported): BRTA official count is police-FIR based. WHO uses a modelled 30-day standard. The gap is structural.

Step 3 · The Gap

The 5.8× Undercount

The annual disparity between official and epidemiological records is massive: the BRTA logged 5,480 road deaths in 2023, while WHO estimated 31,578 fatalities. Dividing the modelled estimate by the official registry produces a ratio of 5.76, rounded to 5.8. Bangladesh's roads kill at nearly six times the official police record.

Key Finding (Claim R-02 · Supported): WHO modelled deaths (31,578) are 5.8× the BRTA official count (5,480). Ratio: 31,578 ÷ 5,480 = 5.76×.

Step 4 · Dataset Audit

Cleaning 95% Artificial Padding

Investigating raw road crash data requires rigorous audit. The bureau examined a raw Figshare crash repository containing 47,680 rows and identified that 95.11% of the file consisted of synthetic padding created by cross-joining identical accidents across metadata fields. Auditing by nine identifying fields reduced the file to 2,331 unique crashes.

Key Finding (Claim R-06 · Confirmed): 47,680 raw rows cleaned to 2,331 unique accidents. 95.11% was synthetic padding. One-record variance (2,331 vs. 2,332) flagged unresolved.

Step 6 · Night Window

The 10pm–6am Window: 41.56% of Fatal Crashes

Temporal distribution analysis shows that 41.56% of fatal crashes in Dhaka occur during the eight-hour window between 10pm and 6am. This concentration coincides directly with the municipal window when long-haul heavy trucks enter the capital's arterial avenues, combined with unpoliced nighttime speeds. Pedestrians comprise 47% of all road victims.

Key Finding (Claim R-07 · Confirmed): 41.56% of fatal crashes occur at night (10pm–6am). Pedestrians account for 47% of victims. Corrected 2026-06-08 (C-001).

Step 7 · The Contract

The Joma System: Economic Drivers of Speed

Under the joma daily-lease system, a private owner leases the bus to a driver-helper team for a fixed daily fee before fuel, helper wages, and gate fees: BDT 4,000–6,000 a day, rising to Tk 8,000 or more during Eid. Only after those costs are covered does the driver start earning personal income — BDT 300–500 a day, on the Transparency International Bangladesh 2024 figures cited in the live report. These figures are TIB estimates, not independently replicated by the bureau. A driver who must pay first and earn later is responding to a contract that prices caution as a loss, forcing teams to race competing buses for passenger fares.

Key Finding (Claim R-03 · Confirmed): The joma daily-lease system forces bus drivers to race for passengers, because drivers pay a fixed daily fee before earning (BDT 4,000–6,000/day, rising to Tk 8,000 on Eid; driver net income BDT 300–500).

Step 8 · The Return

The Phoenix Pattern: Banned Fleets Rebranded

Regulatory enforcement routinely fails against fleet continuity. Following a fatal student collision in 2019, the High Court and BRTA banned Suprobhat Paribahan from the Sadarghat–Gazipur corridor. The physical bus chassis returned to the same route repainted under new company names, including Victor Classic and Akash Paribahan. Route continuity is probable based on permits; corporate ownership continuity is alleged but unconfirmed in public registries.

Key Finding (Claim R-04 · Contested): Banned fleets return under new names on identical corridors. Route continuity is probable; ownership continuity remains unverified in company registries.

Step 9 · The Four Voids

Four Government Datasets Held, Not Published

Institutional accountability is obstructed by four deliberate information voids in government records. The state maintains records that could settle road safety controversies but does not publish them: (1) BRTA chassis-level crash registry, (2) hospital emergency trauma records linked to crash causes, (3) police FIRs linked to corporate operator names, and (4) route-permit beneficial ownership registries.

Key Finding (Claim R-05 · Confirmed): Four key datasets are held by transport and health authorities but withheld from the public.
