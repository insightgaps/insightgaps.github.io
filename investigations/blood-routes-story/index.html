Blood Routes, A Story

This is a pilot, told in the voice of a narrator of the case, not a lecturer. The story carries the engagement. You carry the judgment. At each decision point the narrator stops. Decide first, then open the reveal. Nothing in the story is new: every number, claim, and correction below comes from the published visual-first report for Blood Routes, BD-INV-001, and its claim register. Where the report says a figure is reported rather than re-computed, or a chain is probable rather than confirmed, this story says so too.

Scene 1 · The rush

Scene 1 · The rush

It begins, as it does every year, with everyone trying to leave at once. Buses fill before dawn. The road out of Dhaka is not a road that week. It is a queue, a market, and a race, all at the same time.

Then the counting starts, after the fact, one body at a time, from reports that were never designed to add up. During the 15-day Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 travel period, 351 people were killed — about 23 per day. That is the headline, and the narrator will not dress it up. It is also, in a precise sense, a borrowed headline.

The 351 deaths are reported, not re-computed. They come from Road Safety Foundation and Bangladesh Jatri Kalyan Samity Eid monitoring, as published in the live report, and the underlying reports are not yet archived in the bureau's evidence repository. The cleaned dataset behind the rest of this story covers 2022–2024. The Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 rush sits outside it. So this story can tell you what was reported — 351 divided by 15 is 23.4 per day, rounded to 23 — but it cannot show you its own recount of those 15 days. That distinction is the first test of whether you will trust a number because it is large, or because you know how it was made.

Eid-ul-Adha tells the same warning in a smaller, checkable form: deaths rose from 262 in 2024 to 312 in 2025, a 19% rise. It was once described as a 29% surge. Correction C-002, dated 2026-09-03, corrected it. A story that corrects its own surge in public asks you to believe the method more than the headline.

Decision point 1 — decide first: would you publish 351 as a fact, or as a reported figure?

Reveal — Claim R-01 · Supported. During the 15-day Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 travel period, 351 people were killed, averaging 23 deaths per day. Grade: Supported, not Confirmed, precisely because the period is outside the cleaned dataset's range and the underlying reports are not yet archived. The Eid-ul-Adha correction, C-002, is part of the same discipline: 312 vs 262 is a 19% rise, not a 29% surge.

Scene 2 · The counting choice

Scene 2 · The counting choice

Now stand in the counting room, where there are two ledgers and no one will tell you which one to trust. On one desk, the Bangladesh Road Transport Authority counts 5,480 road deaths a year. On the other, the World Health Organization models 31,578. They are not counting the same thing, and that is the point. The official tally counts only incidents logged in police First Information Reports. A victim who dies on the way to hospital, or whose family settles out of court, is not in it. The WHO model uses a 30-day, modelled definition: a death counted if it follows a crash within that window, whether or not a police file was ever opened.

Divide one by the other and you get 5.76, rounded to 5.8. The story does not ask you to pick the bigger number because it is more dramatic. It asks you to notice what a definition does: change the definition, and the total changes by a factor of almost six. That is a finding about the system that counts, not a rounding difference.

Decision point 2 — decide first: what would you count?

Would you count only deaths the police recorded at the scene, or every death within 30 days of a crash, even if no file was opened? There is no neutral answer. The first count is tidy and incomplete. The second is fuller and modelled, which means it rests on assumptions you must also be willing to examine.

Reveal — Claim R-02 · Supported. The WHO-modelled estimate, about 31,578 per year, is 5.8 times the BRTA official count of 5,480. The gap is knowable and publishable, and the definitions behind it are stated above, not hidden.

Before / after: official count vs modelled count

Before, official only: 5,480 deaths a year, police FIR deaths only. After, with the modelled definition added: 31,578 deaths a year, 30-day modelled definition. The difference is not an error to be averaged away. It is the distance between two definitions, and the story keeps both on the table.

Scene 3 · The file that lied

Scene 3 · The file that lied

Every investigation has a moment when the file itself becomes a character, and this one misbehaves beautifully. The bureau opened a dataset of 47,680 rows and found, after grouping rows by nine identifying fields and aggregating counts by maximum per incident, that only 2,331 were unique accidents. About 95% of the raw rows were artificial padding, created by cross-joining single accidents across permuted metadata values.

Only after the cleaning could anyone honestly say where crashes cluster. In the deduplicated dataset, they cluster at highway entry and exit corridors and roundabouts: Tejgaon Industrial Area, 8; Hazrat Shahjalal Airport Roundabout, 6; Jatrabari Intersection, 6; Mayor Mohammad Hanif Flyover, 5; Shonir Akhra on the Dhaka–Chittagong Highway, 4. The story names them because the cleaned file, not the padded one, earned the right to name them.

The same cleaning corrects a second claim. The night was once reported as almost the whole story: 97.13% of fatal Dhaka crashes, it was said, happened at night. That figure came from a dataset later found to contain about 95% synthetic padding, and it was retracted on 2026-06-03. Correction C-001, dated 2026-06-08, puts the corrected share at 41.56% between 10pm and 6am. Pedestrians account for 47% of Dhaka road victims. The night is still an enforcement void. It is no longer, on this evidence, the only void.

One loose thread stays loose. The live scene heading says 2,332. This report uses 2,331, the audited count, and flags the difference as unresolved.

Decision point 3 — decide first: would you trust a hotspot drawn from the raw file?

Reveal — Claim R-06 · Confirmed, and Claim R-07 · Confirmed. The deduplicated dataset shows heavy clustering at corridors and roundabouts, as tabled above. Fatal Dhaka crashes between 10pm and 6am are 41.56%, corrected from 97.13% by C-001, and pedestrians are 47% of Dhaka road victims.

Before / after: raw rows vs deduplicated accidents

Before, raw: 47,680 rows. After, deduplicated: 2,331 unique accidents. The 95.11% that disappeared was padding, not crashes. Any hotspot, night share, or trend drawn before that cleaning would have been drawn from padding as much as from crashes.

Scene 3, continued · Where it happens

Keep this chart beside the night chart, not instead of it. Corridors tell you where. Night tells you when enforcement thins. Neither tells you why a driver was in a hurry. For that, the story has to get into the bus.

Scene 4 · The contract

Scene 4 · The contract

Picture the morning handover. A private owner leases the bus to a driver-helper team for a fixed daily fee, before fuel, helper wages, and gate fees: BDT 4,000–6,000 a day, rising to Tk 8,000 or more during Eid. Only after those costs are covered does the driver start earning personal income — BDT 300–500 a day, on the Transparency International Bangladesh 2024 figures cited in the live report.

Now ask the question the story has been building toward, before you blame the man at the wheel. If your day only starts paying after you have already paid to work, and every passenger you pick up before the next bus is money you keep, what does the contract reward? It rewards getting there first, racing for passengers, speed over the timetable — because there is no salary to fall back on, only the next fare.

These figures are TIB estimates, not independently replicated by the bureau, and the story says so. That caveat does not weaken the scene. A driver who must pay first and earn later is not being careless in a vacuum. He is responding, rationally, to a contract that prices caution as a loss.

Decision point 4 — decide first: what does this contract reward?

If you answered “racing”, you have the claim.

Reveal — Claim R-03 · Confirmed. The joma daily-lease system forces bus drivers to race for passengers, because drivers pay a fixed daily fee before earning. The figures are attributed to TIB, not independently replicated by the bureau.

Scene 5 · The return

Scene 5 · The return · Claim R-04 · Contested

Every good story has a return, and this one has a ghost. After a fatal crash in 2019, the High Court and BRTA banned Suprobhat Paribahan from the Sadarghat–Gazipur corridor. That should have been an ending. The live report documents the same physical chassis continuing under shell cooperative names, including Victor Classic and Akash Paribahan, on identical routes.

Here the narrator must slow down, because this is where a story can do real harm if it runs ahead of its evidence. Route continuity is probable. Ownership continuity is alleged, not confirmed by a corporate registry. No beneficial ownership is asserted, and no operator is described as guilty unless a specific conviction is cited. The pattern is called the Phoenix pattern: a banned fleet re-emerging under new operator names on the same routes. The name is vivid. The grade is Contested. Both facts have to stay in the same sentence, or the story is lying by emphasis.

So the narrator asks you: a bus you banned is back, under a new name, on the same road. Do you publish the chain as fact, or as a chain that still needs the one document that would settle it?

Decision point 5 — decide first: would you publish this chain?

Reveal — Claim R-04 · Contested. Banned fleets re-emerge under new operator names on the same routes. Route continuity is probable; ownership continuity is alleged, not confirmed by a corporate registry. Sources: live report, Phoenix scene — High Court and BRTA ban on Suprobhat Paribahan, Sadarghat–Gazipur corridor, 2019 crash context; family and victim testimony for ownership continuity. No operator is found guilty unless a specific conviction is cited.

If you would not publish ownership as fact on this evidence, you have understood why the grade is Contested.

Scene 6 · The voids

VOID-01 · Chassis registry

BRTA bus crash registry by vehicle chassis — would confirm or deny Phoenix ownership chains. Held, classified, not published.

VOID-02 · Hospital records

Hospital trauma records linked to crash cause — the only ground truth for the 5.8× undercount. Paper, undigitised, unconnected.

VOID-03 · Operator-linked FIRs

Police FIRs linked to operator name. FIRs record registration numbers, not operator brands.

VOID-04 · Beneficial owners

Route-permit beneficial-owner registry. Many permits are held by front companies or politically connected individuals.

Scene 6 · The voids · Claim R-05 · Confirmed

The story ends, as investigations should, not with a villain unmasked but with four empty shelves. Four datasets the government holds are not published in the form needed to test what this story has claimed: a BRTA bus crash registry by chassis, which would confirm or deny a Phoenix ownership chain; hospital trauma records linked to crash cause, the only ground truth for the 5.8× undercount, still paper, undigitised, unconnected; police FIRs linked to operator name — FIRs record registration numbers, not operator brands; and a route-permit beneficial-owner registry, where many permits are held by front companies or politically connected individuals.

Each absence is a documented accountability gap, not a technical limitation. Publishing those four datasets would test the Phoenix chain and the undercount directly. Not publishing them leaves both exactly where this story leaves them: probable, alleged, reported, supported — and unfinished.

That is why the narrator stops here instead of closing the case. The reforms proposed in the live report, not findings, are: abolish joma in favour of monthly salary contracts; publish a chassis-linked registry; enforce owner accountability under the Road Transport Act 2018; and create an independent accident investigation body. They are proposals. The findings are the ones you have already decided on, scene by scene.

Decision point 6 — decide first: what would you ask for first?

Would you ask first for the chassis registry, to settle the Phoenix chain, or for linked hospital records, to settle the undercount? Either answer is defensible. Claiming either question is already answered is not.

Reveal — Claim R-05 · Confirmed. Four datasets the government holds are not published: BRTA bus crash registry by chassis, hospital trauma records linked to crash cause, police FIRs linked to operator name, and a route-permit beneficial-owner registry. This is a confirmed absence finding: each dataset is held or should exist and is not published in the form needed.

What we do not know

  • Eid-2026 headline period is outside the cleaned dataset's range. The cleaned dataset covers 2,331 unique accidents in 2022–2024.
  • The 351 Eid-2026 deaths are reported, not re-computed. They come from Road Safety Foundation and Bangladesh Jatri Kalyan Samity reporting, and the underlying reports are not yet archived in the bureau's evidence repository.
  • Operator attribution is not a registry fact. Police and government databases systematically omit operator names.
  • Live-report operator names are assigned by regional routing rules in the internal analysis, and its map provenance note states operator names are not assigned in the public download because the crash log does not carry a reliable operator field.
  • Whether publishing the four held datasets would confirm or deny any Phoenix ownership chain — unknown.
  • What share of crashes are settled before police — unknown.
  • How many victims die after hospital admission — unknown, because hospital deaths are not linked to crash records.
  • The 2,332 vs 2,331 difference — the live scene heading says 2,332; this pilot uses 2,331, the audited count, and flags the difference as unresolved.

What to watch next

  • Whether the four held datasets are published — the BRTA chassis registry, linked hospital trauma records, operator-linked FIRs, and the beneficial-owner registry.
  • Whether the Eid-2026 underlying reports are archived in the bureau's evidence repository, so the 351 figure can be re-computed rather than reported.
  • Whether a fresh, logged reply attempt is made before any new allegation beyond what the live report already published — BRTA and the Phoenix operators have no recorded reply in the appendix table.
  • Whether the flagged inconsistencies are resolved — the 2,332 vs 2,331 scene-heading difference, and the manifest revised date of 2026-06-08 vs the 2026-09-03 corrections.
