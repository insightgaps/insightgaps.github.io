The story in ten steps

Step 1 · Decide first

The rush

It begins, as it does every year, with everyone trying to leave at once. Buses fill before dawn. The road out of Dhaka is not a road that week. It is a queue, a market, and a race, all at the same time. Then the counting starts, after the fact, one body at a time, from reports that were never designed to add up.

During the 15-day Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 travel period, 351 people were killed — about 23 per day. That is the headline, and this story will not dress it up. But the first thing you need to know is what kind of number it is. It comes from Road Safety Foundation and Bangladesh Jatri Kalyan Samity Eid monitoring, as published in the live report, and the underlying reports are not yet archived in the bureau's evidence repository. The cleaned dataset behind the rest of this story covers 2022 to 2024. The Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 rush sits outside it. So this story can tell you what was reported. It cannot show you its own recount of those 15 days.

Decision point 1 — decide first: would you publish 351 as a fact, or as a reported figure?

Reveal — Claim R-01 · Supported. During the 15-day Eid-ul-Fitr 2026 travel period, 351 people were killed, averaging 23 deaths per day. The grade is Supported, not Confirmed, precisely because the period is outside the cleaned dataset's range and the underlying reports are not yet archived. The arithmetic is checkable — 351 divided by 15 is 23.4 per day, rounded to 23 — but arithmetic on a reported total does not turn it into a recount.

Step 2 · Decide first

Two definitions

Now stand in the counting room, where there are two ledgers and no one will tell you which one to trust. The Bangladesh Road Transport Authority counts only incidents logged in police First Information Reports. A victim who dies on the way to hospital is not in it. A family that settles out of court is not in it. How many victims die after hospital admission is not known at all, because hospital deaths are not linked to crash records.

The World Health Organization counts differently: a death within 30 days of a crash, modelled, whether or not a police file was ever opened. Neither ledger is lying. They are answering different questions, and the distance between their answers is the story. Before you see the two totals, decide what you think a road death count should include — because the answer you give here decides which total you will believe in the next step.

Decision point 2 — decide first: what would you count?

Would you count only deaths the police recorded, or every death within 30 days of a crash, even if no file was opened? There is no neutral answer. The first count is tidy and incomplete. The second is fuller and modelled, which means it rests on assumptions you must also be willing to examine. The reveal in the next step gives both totals; this step's claim is the definitions behind them.

Reveal — Claim R-02 · Supported (definitions). The BRTA official count is a police-FIR count. The WHO estimate uses a modelled 30-day definition. The gap between them is a definition gap first and a number second.

Step 3 · Decide first

The gap

Here are the totals. The official count is 5,480 road deaths a year. The modelled estimate is 31,578. Divide one by the other and you get 5.76, rounded to 5.8. Say it plainly: the modelled figure is nearly six times the official one. This story does not ask you to pick the bigger number because it is more dramatic. It asks you to notice what a definition does. Change the definition, and the total changes by a factor of almost six. That is a finding about the system that counts, not a rounding difference.

And the gap is knowable and publishable. Both figures are published, both definitions are stated, and the division is shown in the final step for anyone with a calculator.

Decision point 3 — decide first: before you open the reveal, which total would you headline?

If you headline only 5,480, you publish a tidy count you know is incomplete. If you headline only 31,578, you publish a model as if it were a count. If you headline both, with their definitions, you publish something harder to quote and harder to dismiss.

Reveal — Claim R-02 · Supported. The WHO-modelled road death estimate, about 31,578 per year, is 5.8 times the BRTA official count of 5,480. Ratio: 31,578 divided by 5,480 equals 5.76, rounded to 5.8. Definitions differ, as set out in Step 2.

Step 4 · Decide first

The file that lied

Every investigation has a moment when the file itself becomes a character, and this one misbehaves beautifully. The bureau opened a dataset of 47,680 rows and found that only 2,331 were unique accidents. About 95% of the raw rows were artificial padding, created by cross-joining single accidents across permuted metadata values. This is the one method sentence the story cannot tell without, so here it is, labelled as method: rows were grouped by nine identifying fields and counts were aggregated by maximum per incident.

Only after that cleaning could anyone honestly say where crashes cluster, or when they happen. Any hotspot, night share, or trend drawn before the cleaning would have been drawn from padding as much as from crashes. And one loose thread stays loose: the live scene heading says 2,332. This story uses 2,331, the audited count, and flags the one-record difference as unresolved. It does not pick the tidier number and move on.

Decision point 4 — decide first: would you trust a hotspot drawn from the raw file?

Reveal — Claim R-06 · Confirmed (cleaning). The deduplicated dataset — 47,680 raw rows reduced to 2,331 unique accidents, because 95.11% of raw rows were artificial padding — is the only file in this story that has earned the right to name a hotspot. The 2,332 vs 2,331 difference is flagged, not smoothed.

Step 5 · Decide first

Where it happens

Now, and only now, the story names places. In the deduplicated dataset, crashes cluster at highway entry and exit corridors and roundabouts: Tejgaon Industrial Area, 8; Hazrat Shahjalal Airport Roundabout, 6; Jatrabari Intersection, 6; Mayor Mohammad Hanif Flyover, 5; Shonir Akhra on the Dhaka–Chittagong Highway, 4. These are the places where enforcement, design, and speed meet.

But read the total before you read the ranking. Those five places together account for 29 crashes. A bar chart drawn at full width makes 8 look large and 4 look small; the honest reading is that both are small counts from a cleaned file, and the pattern worth trusting is the kind of place — corridors and roundabouts — not the exact order of five small numbers.

Decision point 5 — decide first: would you publish this as a ranked hotspot list?

Reveal — Claim R-06 · Confirmed (clustering). The deduplicated dataset shows heavy clustering at highway entry and exit corridors and roundabouts, as tabled. The claim is the clustering by type of place, not a precise ranking: the counts are 8, 6, 6, 5, and 4, totalling 29.

Step 6 · Decide first

The night

Keep the corridor chart beside this one, not instead of it. Corridors tell you where. Night tells you when enforcement thins. Of fatal Dhaka crashes, 41.56% occur between 10pm and 6am, and pedestrians account for 47% of Dhaka road victims. The night is an enforcement void. On this evidence, it is no longer the only void — and that correction is itself part of the story.

The night was once reported as almost the whole story: 97.13% of fatal Dhaka crashes, it was said, happened at night. That figure came from a dataset later found to contain about 95% synthetic padding, and it was retracted on 2026-06-03. Correction C-001, dated 2026-06-08, puts the corrected share at 41.56%. The other bar in the chart, 58.44% for the rest of the day, is not a measurement. It is arithmetic: 100 minus 41.56. A story that corrects its own night in public asks you to believe the method more than the headline.

Decision point 6 — decide first: after a retraction like that, do you trust the corrected 41.56%?

Reveal — Claim R-07 · Confirmed. 41.56% of fatal Dhaka crashes occur between 10pm and 6am, and pedestrians account for 47% of Dhaka road victims (Road Safety Foundation, as corrected 2026-06-08, correction C-001). Trust here is not blind: the retraction is dated, the old figure is named, and the reason — padding in the source dataset — is the same padding Step 4 already showed you.

Step 7 · Decide first

The contract

Picture the morning handover. A private owner leases the bus to a driver-helper team for a fixed daily fee, before fuel, helper wages, and gate fees: BDT 4,000–6,000 a day, rising to Tk 8,000 or more during Eid. Only after those costs are covered does the driver start earning personal income — BDT 300–500 a day, on the Transparency International Bangladesh 2024 figures cited in the live report. These figures are TIB estimates, not independently replicated by the bureau.

Now ask the question the story has been building toward, before you blame the man at the wheel. If your day only starts paying after you have already paid to work, and every passenger you pick up before the next bus is money you keep, what does the contract reward? There is no salary to fall back on, only the next fare. A driver who must pay first and earn later is not being careless in a vacuum. He is responding, rationally, to a contract that prices caution as a loss.

Decision point 7 — decide first: what does this contract reward?

Reveal — Claim R-03 · Confirmed. The joma daily-lease system forces bus drivers to race for passengers, because drivers pay a fixed daily fee before earning. The figures are attributed to TIB, not independently replicated by the bureau. That caveat does not weaken the scene; it states exactly how far the evidence goes.

Step 8 · Decide first

The return

Every good story has a return, and this one has a ghost. After a fatal crash in 2019, the High Court and BRTA banned Suprobhat Paribahan from the Sadarghat–Gazipur corridor. That should have been an ending. The live report documents the same physical chassis continuing under shell cooperative names, including Victor Classic and Akash Paribahan, on identical routes.

Here the narrator must slow down, because this is where a story can do real harm if it runs ahead of its evidence. A bus you banned is back, under a new name, on the same road. The route continuity is probable. The ownership continuity is alleged, not confirmed by a corporate registry — it rests on family and victim testimony, not on a registry entry. No beneficial ownership is asserted, and no operator is described as guilty unless a specific conviction is cited. Hold both halves of that sentence together, or the story is lying by emphasis.

Decision point 8 — decide first: would you publish this chain as fact?

Reveal — Claim R-04 · Contested. Banned fleets re-emerge under new operator names on the same routes — the Phoenix pattern. Route continuity is probable; ownership continuity is alleged, not confirmed by a corporate registry. Sources: live report, Phoenix scene — High Court and BRTA ban on Suprobhat Paribahan, Sadarghat–Gazipur corridor, 2019 crash context; family and victim testimony for ownership continuity. If you would not publish ownership as fact on this evidence, you have understood why the grade is Contested.

Step 9 · Decide first

The voids

The story ends, as investigations should, not with a villain unmasked but with four empty shelves. Four datasets the government holds are not published in the form needed to test what this story has claimed. A BRTA bus crash registry by chassis would confirm or deny a Phoenix ownership chain. Hospital trauma records linked to crash cause are the only ground truth for the 5.8× undercount — still paper, undigitised, unconnected. Police FIRs linked to operator name do not exist in usable form, because FIRs record registration numbers, not operator brands. And a route-permit beneficial-owner registry would show who really holds the permits, where many are held by front companies or politically connected individuals.

Each absence is a documented accountability gap, not a technical limitation. Publishing those four datasets would test the Phoenix chain and the undercount directly.

Decision point 9 — decide first: what would you ask for first — and what stays untested while you wait?

This is a prioritisation, not a verdict, and the reveal says so honestly. Either answer is defensible. Claiming either question is already answered is not.

Reveal — Claim R-05 · Confirmed. Four datasets the government holds are not published: BRTA bus crash registry by chassis, hospital trauma records linked to crash cause, police FIRs linked to operator name, and a route-permit beneficial-owner registry. This is a confirmed absence finding: each dataset is held or should exist and is not published in the form needed. Ask for the chassis registry first and the undercount stays untested; ask for hospital records first and the Phoenix chain stays alleged.

Step 10 · Decide first

The check

Before the close, redo the story's arithmetic with a calculator. Thirty-one thousand five hundred seventy-eight divided by 5,480 is 5.76, rounded to 5.8. Three hundred fifty-one divided by 15 is 23.4, rounded to 23. Eid-ul-Adha deaths rose from 262 in 2024 to 312 in 2025: that is a 19.08% rise, rounded to 19% — and it was once described as a 29% surge. Correction C-002, dated 2026-09-03, corrected it. A story that corrects its own surge in public asks you to believe the method more than the headline.

The reforms proposed in the live report are proposals, not findings: abolish joma in favour of monthly salary contracts; publish a chassis-linked registry; enforce owner accountability under the Road Transport Act 2018; create an independent accident investigation body. The findings are the ones you have already decided on, step by step. And the story closes where it must: probable, alleged, reported, supported — and unfinished. Until the four datasets are published, until the Eid-2026 reports are archived so the 351 can be re-computed, and until the one-record difference between 2,332 and 2,331 is resolved, this case is not closed. It is only, honestly, told.

Decision point 10 — decide first: did the arithmetic hold, and what did it not prove?

The arithmetic held, every division above. What it did not prove is the part no division can prove: that the 351 were counted the same way twice, that the Phoenix chain is an ownership chain, or that a published dataset would confirm either claim. Calculations check the story's sums. Only the four held datasets can check its chains.

Reveal — corrections and grades, together. C-001 (2026-06-08): night share corrected from 97.13% to 41.56%. C-002 (2026-09-03): Eid-ul-Adha description corrected from a 29% surge to a 19% rise, 312 vs 262. Grades: R-01 Supported, R-02 Supported, R-03 Confirmed, R-04 Contested, R-05 Confirmed, R-06 Confirmed, R-07 Confirmed — exactly as in the claim register BD-INV-001.json.

What we do not know

  • Eid-2026 headline period is outside the cleaned dataset's range. The cleaned dataset covers 2,331 unique accidents in 2022–2024.
  • The 351 Eid-2026 deaths are reported, not re-computed. They come from Road Safety Foundation and Bangladesh Jatri Kalyan Samity reporting, and the underlying reports are not yet archived in the bureau's evidence repository.
  • Operator attribution is not a registry fact. Police and government databases systematically omit operator names.
  • Live-report operator names are assigned by regional routing rules in the internal analysis, and its map provenance note states operator names are not assigned in the public download because the crash log does not carry a reliable operator field.
  • The 10-year map is a distribution, not a decade of observed points. The raw Figshare dataset's Year column was decoupled padding; true incident dates are 2022–2024.
  • That distribution spreads the 2,331 deduplicated accidents proportionally to annual Jatri Kalyan Samity fatalities. That construction must be labelled as such wherever the map is shown.
  • Whether publishing the four held datasets would confirm or deny any Phoenix ownership chain — unknown.
  • What share of crashes are settled before police — unknown.
  • How many victims die after hospital admission — unknown, because hospital deaths are not linked to crash records.
  • The 2,332 vs 2,331 difference — the live scene heading says 2,332; this draft uses 2,331, the audited count, and flags the difference as unresolved.

What to watch next

  • Whether the four held datasets are published — the BRTA chassis registry, linked hospital trauma records, operator-linked FIRs, and the beneficial-owner registry.
  • Whether the Eid-2026 underlying reports are archived in the bureau's evidence repository, so the 351 figure can be re-computed rather than reported.
  • Whether a fresh, logged reply attempt is made before any new allegation beyond what the live report already published — BRTA and the Phoenix operators have no recorded reply in the appendix table.
  • Whether the flagged inconsistencies are resolved — the 2,332 vs 2,331 scene-heading difference, and the manifest revised date of 2026-06-08 vs the 2026-09-03 corrections.
