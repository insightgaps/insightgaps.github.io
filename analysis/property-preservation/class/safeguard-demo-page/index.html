Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Quick way back in: a family stops paying — that's default. Before anything can be taken, help must be tried first — that stage is called loss mitigation, and its named exits are a pause or smaller payments (forbearance), new loan terms (a modification), selling for less than is owed with the lender's agreement (a short sale), or handing the deed back instead of fighting (deed-in-lieu). Only when those fail does foreclosure begin — and foreclosure is state law, a court lawsuit in some states, a trustee sale in others. Then an auction. Then, when no buyer tops the lender's bid, the house becomes bank-owned — REO — or, on a government-insured loan, is handed to the insuring agency. That is the whole road. She is standing at the end of it.

How to run the recall, step by step

  1. Ask for the first step after a missed payment. Take answers cold — no correcting yet.
  2. Push on the order, not just the list: “What has to be tried before foreclosure can even start?”
  3. If a step is missing, don't lecture it back. Ask the question that leads there: “The house is sold — who usually ends up owning it?”
  4. Close it in under eight minutes, standing at the empty house. That is where today begins.

If you've done this work before

You already know why the order matters in the field: the road decides what state a house is in when it reaches you, and what state it is in decides what may be done to it. A house mid-road and a house at the end of it can look identical from the street. The recall isn't revision — it's the habit of asking “where on the road is she?” before asking “what's the job?”

The line to remember: “Default is the first page, not the last — and the road has exits most people never hear about.”

If this is all new to you

Three of the numbers above sound alike and count completely different things. A filing is a paper step — a notice entering the system. A foreclosure start is a process beginning. A repossession is a home actually taken. Millions of filings became far fewer repossessions, because the road has exits and many families used them. Quote a filing count as homes taken and you have multiplied the disaster — and misread the industry you're joining.

How the three counts relate, step by step

  1. A filing lands — the widest count, the most houses touched on paper.
  2. Some filings become starts; some families cure the loan or exit through loss mitigation.
  3. Some starts reach auction; some are stopped, settled, or sold along the way.
  4. What is left at the end is repossessions — the smallest count, and the only one that means a home changed hands.

Chapter 2 · The question this class answers

She stands empty. Who looks after her now?

One house is a job. Ten thousand houses, in fifty states, is a system. The loan on her is managed by a servicer — the company that collects the payments and minds the loan for whoever owns it. When the loan went bad and the house fell empty, the servicer was the one left holding the problem.

Nobody is paid to care about her — yet. By the end of this class, a whole industry will stand between her and the weather.

Say it in class

“She stands empty. Who looks after her now? One house is a job. Ten thousand houses, in fifty states, is a system. Today you meet the system.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Picture the servicer's side of the desk. Their houses are everywhere at once — a frozen pipe in Minnesota, a broken window in Florida, an overgrown lawn in Arizona, all on the same morning. To do that work themselves they would need their own crews in fifty states: fifty sets of weather, fifty sets of local rules, fifty labour markets, and thousands of small towns that no office can reach in a day. Staffing all of that directly would cost more than the work is worth — and it would still be too slow. So the problem isn't finding one crew. It's that the problem is shaped like a whole country.

How the problem takes shape, step by step

  1. The loan goes bad; the house falls empty; the servicer holds the problem.
  2. The same week, the same thing happens in dozens of other states.
  3. Each house needs eyes, locks, lawns, and proof — quickly, before vacancy itself does damage.
  4. No single office can staff that map. The work has to be handed to companies built to cover ground.

If you've done this work before

Hold onto the shape of this problem, because it explains everything downstream — including why your desk exists. Every hand the work passes through exists because the map is too big for the pair of hands before it. When a trainee later asks “why can't the client just deal with us directly?”, the honest answer is on this slide: scale. The chain isn't bureaucracy. It's geography.

The line to remember: “The problem is shaped like a whole country — so the answer had to be, too.”

If this is all new to you

A lived-in house maintains itself in a hundred small ways — heat running, water moving, eyes on every room. Take the family away and the house starts falling, slowly and then quickly. The lawn overgrows and the mail piles up, quietly announcing to the whole street that nobody is home. A small leak or a broken seal lets the weather in. Damp becomes mould. And once a house looks empty long enough, people notice — fixtures and copper have a way of walking out of houses nobody watches. Somebody has to be accountable for a property nobody lives in. That sentence is the seed this entire industry grew from.

How an empty house falls, step by step

  1. Week one: the lawn overgrows, mail piles up — the house starts signalling vacancy.
  2. Weeks two to four: a small leak or broken seal lets water and weather start their work.
  3. Month two: damp becomes mould; small damage becomes big damage.
  4. Month three onward: vandals notice, fixtures and copper go missing, and the house slides from lived-in to blighted.

If you've done this work before

You've seen every stage of this list from a doorstep. The teaching point worth saying out loud: speed is not rudeness in this industry, it's the product. The gap between “house falls empty” and “someone accountable is watching it” is where most of the value is either saved or lost — and it's the gap the system on the next slides was built to close.

The line to remember: “Vacancy itself is damage. The clock starts the day the key stops turning.”

Chapter 3 · How the work travels

The work has to travel down a chain.

So the work gets outsourced — a plain word for a plain idea: instead of doing the work yourself, you hire other companies to do it for you. The servicer hires companies that cover whole regions. Those companies hire the local crews who actually drive to the house. And the proof travels back up the same chain, hand over hand.

That chain is not an accident, and it is not laziness. It is structural — built into the shape of the job itself.

Say it in class

“Outsourcing is not a choice somebody made one morning. It is the shape of the job. Fifty states, thousands of towns — the work has to travel down a chain.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Read the chain from left to right and you are watching a job being born. The loan owner holds the debt but never touches the house. The servicer manages the loan and carries the empty-house problem. The national — the kind of company this class is about — takes the work for the whole country and splits it by region. Regional companies hand it to local crews, and a crew finally drives to the house, changes the locks, cuts the grass, takes the photos. Then everything reverses: the photos, the report, and the invoice climb back up the same chain, checked at every hand, until the servicer can say the work is proven. A job in this industry is really two journeys — work going down, proof coming up.

How one job travels, step by step

  1. The servicer passes the empty house to a national, with rules attached — the order.
  2. The national routes it to the company covering that region.
  3. The regional company dispatches a local crew, who do the work and photograph it.
  4. The proof climbs back: crew to regional, regional to national, national to servicer — checked at every step.
  5. Only when the proof survives the whole climb does the job count as done.

If you've done this work before

Trainees often arrive thinking the chain is something done to them. Reframe it early: the chain is also what protects them. Every hand above theirs is a hand that checked the permission, the scope, and the proof before money moved. When a later class teaches the permission gate and QC, this slide is the map those lessons hang on.

The line to remember: “Work goes down the chain. Proof comes back up it. A job is both journeys.”

Everything above that end is context. Everything at that end is craft. The next classes teach the craft; this class exists so the craft never loses sight of the country-sized problem it answers.

Chapter 5 · A word to handle with care

“Largest” is something a company says about itself.

You will hear Safeguard — and others — called the largest in the industry. Maybe so. But nobody outside the company has checked it, and in this class an unchecked claim stays what it is: marketing, not a fact we teach.

Marketing is what a company says about itself. Evidence is what someone else can check. Learn to hear the difference, and nobody can sell you a number again.

Say it in class

“When you hear a company called the largest, ask one question: who checked? If the answer is ‘the company’ — you are listening to marketing.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Every claim you will ever hear in this industry belongs to one of two piles. Pile one: what a company says about itself — its website, its brochure, its own announcements. That pile is marketing. It might be true; it is not checked. Pile two: what someone with nothing to gain has verified — a court record, a regulator's figure, an audited count. That pile is evidence. “Largest” sits in pile one, because the only one measuring is the company itself. This class doesn't call it a lie. It calls it what it is — and refuses to teach it as a fact.

How to test any claim, step by step

  1. Who says it? If the answer is the company itself, keep one eyebrow up.
  2. Can someone else check it? A record, a filing, a named source — or nothing but the claim?
  3. Does the checker gain if you believe it? A sale, a contract, a reputation?
  4. Three soft answers means marketing. File it as marketing — interesting, possibly true, never load-bearing.

If you've done this work before

This is the processor's reflex, generalised. A photo that claims a room is empty is pile one until someone checks the corners. A fee schedule a stranger quotes you is pile one until the order confirms it. Teach the three questions here, on a harmless company slogan, and the class will reuse them on numbers that carry money — which is where this industry keeps its expensive mistakes.

The line to remember: “Marketing is what a company says about itself. Evidence is what someone else can check.”

What isn't proven — said out loud

The programme's fact-check keeps “largest” exactly where this slide puts it: company marketing, unverified. No ranking of national firms is taught anywhere in this course, because none has been independently established to the programme's standard. If a trainee arrives carrying a ranking, the kind move is this slide — not an argument.

If this is all new to you

Company slogans are the gentle end of this problem. Further in, you'll meet dramatic numbers passed desk to desk — famous fee collapses, “everyone knows” rates, averages nobody can source. They feel like insider knowledge. Some are true, some were true once, and some were never true at all. The defence is the same three questions, asked without embarrassment: who says it, who checked, who gains. A number that can't survive those questions can't be allowed to steer a bid, a wage expectation, or a career decision.

How an unchecked number does its damage, step by step

  1. Someone repeats a striking figure; it sounds like experience.
  2. It travels desk to desk, gaining confidence at every stop and evidence at none.
  3. Someone finally builds a decision on it — a price, a promise, a plan.
  4. The decision meets the real numbers, and the gap gets paid by whoever trusted the figure.

If you've done this work before

The programme itself models the standard: its research files mark circulating figures as unsupported when the record won't carry them, and refuse to teach them — even figures that would make a lesson more dramatic. That's the professional culture this course is recruiting trainees into. Drama is cheap. A number you can stand behind is the whole job.

The line to remember: “A number nobody checked is just a rumour wearing a suit.”

Chapter 6 · The flood

Then came 2008 — and the flood.

Class 2 ended at the break: price fall plus payment shock, and a family defaults. In 2008 that break happened in millions of houses at once. Foreclosure starts peaked at 2,139,005 in 2009 — and the trickle of empty houses the system was built for became a flood it had to grow to survive.

The flood is the reason this industry matters at the size it does. Everything after this slide is the flood's doing.

Say it in class

“2008 is when the break became a flood — and the flood is what built the system you are about to work inside.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Here is the flood with its labels kept on. Foreclosure starts — processes begun — peaked at 2,139,005 in 2009. Properties receiving at least one filing reached roughly 2.9 million in 2010. Actual repossessions — homes taken — crested at 102,134 in the single worst month, September 2010. And the combined pile of homes in foreclosure plus homes already bank-owned stood at about 2.2 million in December 2010. Four numbers, four different counts. Any one of them, quoted with the wrong label, becomes a small lie — and this class doesn't traffic in those.

How the flood built up, step by step

  1. The loop broke (Class 2): prices fell, payments shocked, defaults multiplied.
  2. 2009: foreclosure starts hit their peak — 2,139,005 processes begun in one year.
  3. 2010: filings touched roughly 2.9 million properties; the road clogged at every step.
  4. September 2010: repossessions peaked at 102,134 in a single month.
  5. December 2010: about 2.2 million homes sat somewhere in the combined pile — in foreclosure, or already bank-owned.

If you've done this work before

One warning from the programme's fact-check, and it belongs in the teacher's mouth, not just this drawer: that 2.2 million is a combined inventory — in-foreclosure plus REO together. Quoted as “REO alone” it invents roughly a million homes that hadn't been taken yet. It's the single most likely number-swap in this whole class, which is why this drawer states it twice.

The line to remember: “Four counts, four labels — the flood is big enough without inflating it.”

Chapter 7 · The other major things

Chapter 9 · Proof learns to travel

Mail. Camera. Portal.

The system runs on proof, and proof has had three lives. First it travelled by mail — printed, enveloped, posted, days in transit. Then it rode home on a camera. Now it lives in a portal — uploaded the same day, attached to the order, checkable at once. Each era hid its gaps for less time than the one before.

Why are there so many photo rules? This slide is the whole answer — the rules grew up around what proof could finally prove.

Spot what is missing

If this is all new to you

Walk the three eras slowly. The mailed print: somebody photographed the house, had it printed, and posted it. Days passed. If the print was dark, or of the wrong room, nobody knew until the envelope arrived — and by then the weather had moved in and the whole slow trip had to happen again. The camera photo: faster, but the pictures sat in the camera until someone carried it home and emptied it; a missing shot could still hide until the day was over. The portal photo: it arrives the same day, in the system, attached to the order, where the before, the during, and the after can be compared side by side.   4. The rules grew to match: what proof can prove, the rulebook now demands.

Chapter 10 · The courtroom

Is changing a lock the same as taking the house?

The class becomes a jury. A crew drives up to an empty house, changes the locks, drives away. Small job — large question: at what moment does touching a house become possession, taking control of it as if it were yours? One state answered it in court: Jordan, decided by the Washington Supreme Court on 7 July 2016.

Vote before the reveal. The vote is the lesson — the answer is only its proof.

The courtroom votes

Hands up before anyone tells you the answer — a crew changes the locks on an empty house. Possession, maintenance, or does it depend on the state? Tap your verdict to see how a strong juror reasons it — then the teacher gives the court's answer, slowly, in order.

Say it in class

“Vote, before I tell you. A crew changes the locks on an empty house. Is that possession — taking control — or is it just maintenance? … The court said possession. The theory was Washington lien theory. The law is Washington only. The settlement came later, and it is not the ruling.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

A lien is a legal claim on a house, held by the lender, while the borrower keeps the deed. Washington works on lien theory: the borrower is treated as the owner, and the lender holds only that claim — not the house. Now the case falls into place. If the borrower is the owner, then someone acting for the lender who changes the locks isn't tidying the lender's property. They are taking control of somebody else's. The Washington Supreme Court said exactly that: under lien theory, a lock change counts as possession. And the label that must never be dropped: this is Washington law only. Other states have other theories, other statutes, other answers.

How the court's reasoning runs, step by step

  1. Washington is a lien-theory state: the borrower owns; the lender holds a claim.
  2. A lock change decides who can enter the house — it is an act of control, not upkeep.
  3. Control exercised for the lender, over the owner's house, is possession.
  4. Therefore: in Washington, changing locks before the lender has the right to possess is stepping over a legal line — permission is not a courtesy, it's the law's shape.

If you've done this work before

Teach Jordan as a Washington answer to a Washington question and it teaches the real lesson — permission: authority first, work second. Teach it as a national ban and you've taught something false in all fifty states at once, including Washington. The judging question to close with: “Tomorrow you are sent to change locks on a house. What do you check first?” Listen for “the order — and the permission.” A trainee who answers “my tools” has understood the tools and missed the job.

The line to remember: “Jordan is a Washington answer to a Washington question. Jurisdiction is the lesson.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

Case and date as carried in the programme's Session 4 materials, where the Washington-only fence is stated every time the case is named.

If this is all new to you

Two different endings attach to this story, and mixing them up is the classic error. The ruling is the court saying what the law means — that's Jordan, 2016. A settlement is something else entirely: the parties agreeing to pay money and stop fighting, without the court deciding anything new. Later, a federal settlement of $17 million was reached in the matter, with final approval on 3 May 2019. Teach the settlement as the settlement and the ruling as the ruling — the moment one stands in for the other, both become wrong.

How the two endings differ, step by step

  1. 2016: the court rules — lock change equals possession, under Washington lien theory. Law is made.
  2. Afterwards: the parties negotiate rather than keep fighting.
  3. 3 May 2019: the $17 million federal settlement wins final approval. The dispute ends; no new law is made.
  4. Two dates, two kinds of event — quote each only for what it is.

If you've done this work before

You've met this exact confusion in paperwork form: an approval is not a payment, a submission is not an acceptance, a quote is not an invoice. Same family of error — two documents that travel together and mean different things. Jordan gives the class a memorable courtroom version of a distinction they'll use weekly at a desk.

The line to remember: “The ruling says what the law means. The settlement says what the fight cost. Never swap them.”
