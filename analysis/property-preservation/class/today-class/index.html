Glossary — 10 terms, one plain sentence each, in teaching order

  1. Bretton Woods (1944): the agreement that steadied world money after the war, so countries could trade and rebuild.
  2. Marshall Plan (1948–52): America's programme that sent money and goods to rebuild war-damaged economies.
  3. GI Bill (1944): the law that helped returning veterans buy houses, with the government guaranteeing part of their home loans.
  4. FHA: the Federal Housing Administration — it insures the loan, it does not lend the money.
  5. Securitisation: bundling many home loans together and selling them as bonds to investors.
  6. Thrift / S&L: a savings-and-loan bank — a local bank that mainly took savings deposits and lent them as home mortgages.
  7. REO: real estate owned — a house the lender now owns after foreclosure.
  8. HUD home: a house the housing department now owns after an insured-loan foreclosure.
  9. Loss mitigation: the lender's ways to help a struggling borrower keep the house or leave it without foreclosure.
  10. Conveyance: the formal handover of the house to the insurer after a foreclosure claim.

The Beginning — complete history (your starting points, all here)

Teach as much of this as you decide — nothing below is trimmed. This is where everything starts.

The war ends, America builds. Factories convert to houses. Bretton Woods 1944 steadies world money. The Marshall Plan 1948–52 rebuilds economies. Veterans come home with savings and a shortage: too few houses, too many families. The GI Bill (22 June 1944; guaranty raised to $4,000 in 1945; VA guaranteed 13.9 million loans by 1993) and FHA insurance — FHA is the Federal Housing Administration, which insures the loan, it does not lend the money — (National Housing Act, 27 June 1934 — long loans replacing short balloon loans with 30–50% down) make a house buyable on a worker's pay. Baby boom: ~76 million births 1946–64. Levittown 1947–51: 17,000+ houses, ~$6,900–$8,000 by model. Interstates 1956. Cars: registrations 25.6 million (1945) → 61.4 million (1960).

More forces behind the rise (all connected, one system): savings-and-loans + deposit insurance funded the loans (thrifts held ~57% of residential mortgages in the late 1960s); single-family zoning decided whole neighbourhoods could hold only detached houses (Berkeley 1916; Euclid 1926; 8 zoning cities in 1916 → 1,246 by 1936); utilities: farms electrified ~11% in 1935 → ~97% in 1960 (farm/rural figures); suburban tracts were wired and plumbed separately by municipalities and developers; air conditioning opened the Sun Belt (share 28% in 1950 → 40% in 2000); the tax code subsidised size (mortgage interest deduction dates to a 1913 accident, not homeownership policy); dual incomes + the 1974 Equal Credit Opportunity Act let two paychecks count; securitisation moved mortgages into bonds (first pass-through year differs across sources — flagged, not settled); houses grew (typical new house under 1,000 sq ft in 1950 → 1,660 in 1973 → 2,631 in 2017); the American Dream gave it meaning (Adams 1931: opportunity, "not a dream of motor cars and high wages merely"). The filter: redlining and restrictive covenants excluded Black families and others from the same boom — prosperity and exclusion, side by side. White flight and school/funding sorting shaped who lived where and which houses later fell vacant. No single cause built the American house; zoning + loans + funding + highways + cars + utilities + AC + tax + culture, with the racial filter, built it together.

The two peaks (never collapse them): TOTAL housing starts peak 1972 = 2,379,000 (1,309,000 single-family + 1,070,000 multifamily — multifamily-led: baby boomers forming households, the 1968 Housing Act's 26-million-unit goal, Section 236 subsidies). SINGLE-FAMILY peak 2005 = 1,716,000 (total 2,068,000 — low average 30-year rate 5.87% in 2005, loosened/subprime lending, securitisation, speculation). Trough 2009 = 554,000. Our house is born in 1972, the biggest TOTAL building year.

The economy loop (teach as a loop, not a list): jobs/income → ability to pay; interest rates → how much house the same payment buys (peak 18.63% Oct 1981; low 2.65% Jan 2021); credit standards + government programmes → who can borrow; building → supply, jobs, prices. When the loop breaks — price fall + payment shock → default → loss mitigation (the lender's ways to help a struggling borrower keep the house or leave it without foreclosure) first (generally no foreclosure start until over 120 days delinquent — verify current text) → foreclosure by state law → auction → REO (real estate owned — a house the lender now owns after foreclosure) or HUD home (a house the housing department now owns after an insured-loan foreclosure) → vacancy → decay → preservation. That loop is why this job exists.

Use the name chosen at entry. Her name goes on every work order, Update Line, and decision from here on. She is sold to a new family at the very end — the story restarts.

1. Grass: does this lawn scream empty?

Task — teacher-led photo read: the teacher points at each photo in turn; trainees call out what proves the house is empty. No one moves on until the class can say it in one sentence.

What it is, plainly: an empty house's lawn keeps growing. Cut it, trim the edges, clear the clippings — so the house looks lived-in, not abandoned. Initial cut = the first, overgrown cut. Recut = the repeat visits that keep it neat.

BEFORE — overgrown, unmowed: Source: uooz.com. What to point at: grass knee-high, edges lost, house disappearing behind the lawn. This is the "before" every grass order starts from.

DURING — the crew at work: Source: naludamagazine.com. What to point at: the worker, the mower, the cut line half done. Proof that someone was there and worked.

AFTER — neat, striped, edged: Source: blogs.aiper.com. What to point at: even cut, clean edges, no clippings left behind. This is "done."

What is required (typical — always check the order): before photos of the full lot; cut + trim + edge; after photos wide (whole lot) and close (edges); date-stamped photos where the client's rulebook requires them (some clients require them, others do not — always check the order). Recut repeats on the order's cycle.

How we handle it: order → permission check (is the house vacant? uncertain = HOLD) → Vendor Manager dispatches the crew → Field Crew cuts, trims, photographs before/during/after → Processor tags + QCs the photos and builds the quantity → Client Manager submits → recut diary set for the next visit.

Teams: Field Crew cuts. Vendor Manager dispatches + confirms the visit. Processor proves + invoices. Client Manager submits + tracks the recut cycle.

Safety: mower and trimmer safety first — eye protection, check the lawn for hidden debris and wires before cutting, never cut toward people or the road.

Cost of getting this wrong: a missed recut or a sloppy after photo means the visit is unpaid and the crew must return — one wasted trip, paid twice in time, earned zero.

SAY: "Grass is the house's face. If the lawn screams empty, everything else is already late."

2. Secure: would you bet this house is shut?

Task — trainees spot the missing photo: show the photos, then ask which lock or board has no photo. No photo, no proof — the class must find the gap before the teacher names it.

What it is, plainly: make the empty house hard to enter and easy to prove. Change every exterior lock, set the lockbox code the order gives, and board any broken or weak entry. Secure first — nothing else happens in an open house.

BEFORE — unsecured, blighted: Source: Manhattan Institute. What to point at: broken, open, inviting trouble. An unsecured house is a liability, not a property.

DURING — rekeying a lock: Source: angi.com. What to point at: hands on the cylinder, pins out. Rekey = the old keys stop working; the new key and the lockbox code are the only way in.

AFTER — boarded and secured: Source: thecleanoutkings.com. What to point at: boards fitted, entries closed, house shut. This is "secured."

What is required (typical — always check the order): every exterior lock changed; lockbox code set exactly as the order states; each broken or weak entry boarded; a photo of every lock and every board, before/during/after, labelled.

How we handle it: order → PERMISSION FIRST (occupancy uncertain = HOLD + escalate, no exceptions — the Jordan lesson: in Washington, changing a lock counted as possession; never assume authority) → Vendor Manager dispatches → Field Crew rekeys + boards + photographs → Processor tags + QCs → Client Manager submits.

Jordan, plainly: possession means acting like you own or control the house — changing its locks is one way to do that. Lien theory means the borrower keeps ownership until foreclosure finishes, while the lender only holds a lien (a legal claim) on the house. In Washington, the state supreme court ruled in 2016 that changing a lock counted as taking possession under that state's lien theory — so a lock change before the lender legally had possession was unlawful there. Washington differs because its lien theory is written into its own state law; other states have different laws, so the same act can be lawful in one state and unlawful in another. That is why the rule is always: permission first, and the governing rulebook is the one on the order.

Teams: Field Crew secures. Vendor Manager owns the permission gate. Processor proves every lock and board. Client Manager submits.

Safety: safety before tidy — never enter a house with occupancy signs, broken glass underfoot, or animals inside; if anything feels occupied or unsafe, STOP + report, do not proceed.

Cost of getting this wrong: one wrong lock change on an occupied house is a wrongful-entry claim — legal cost, client trust lost, and the whole job reversed.

SAY: "Secure is not a job. It is the permission slip for every job after it."

3. Winterize: will winter break this house from inside?

Task — pairs order the steps: pairs put shutoff, drain, antifreeze, and notice in order before the teacher reveals the sequence.

What it is, plainly: water left in pipes freezes, expands, and bursts them. Winterization shuts the water off, drains every line, and puts antifreeze in every trap — so winter cannot break the house from inside.

WHY — frozen pipes, the damage we prevent: Source: itsnotaboutnutrition.com. What to point at: ice in the pipe, the bulge before the burst. One freeze can flood a house.

DURING — draining the lines: Source: diyjoy.com. What to point at: water leaving the system, fixture by fixture. Draining is the job; antifreeze is the backup.

AFTER — winterized and posted: Source: wholehousewaterlab.com. What to point at: dry fixtures, antifreeze visible in traps, notice posted. This is "winterized."

What is required (typical — always check the order): water shut at the main; every line drained; antifreeze in every trap and toilet; water heater handled exactly as the order states; winterization notice posted; a photo of the meter, the shutoff, and each fixture.

How we handle it: order → permission → Vendor Manager dispatches → Field Crew shuts off, drains, antifreezes, photographs each step → Processor tags + QCs (missing fixture photo = send back) → Client Manager submits. Hard rule: if the system will not hold pressure, or a valve is stuck, do NOT fake it — report it as HOLD + problem. A failed test is information, not failure.

Teams: Field Crew winterizes. Vendor Manager dispatches + owns the HOLD rule. Processor proves every fixture. Client Manager submits.

Safety: safety before tidy — hot water can scald, a pressurised line can burst, and antifreeze is poisonous: never taste it, never leave it where children or animals can reach it.

Cost of getting this wrong: one burst pipe can flood a house — the winterization fee is small, the water damage is thousands, and a faked pressure test turns a small job into a liability.

SAY: "Winterization is the cheapest job that prevents the most expensive damage."

4. Debris: can you prove every cubic yard?

Task — CY-maths race: pairs measure the pile from the photos and defend their number; the teacher checks the maths aloud.

What it is, plainly: take everything the order says to take out of the house and yard — old furniture, trash, leftovers — until the house is empty and swept. Measured in cubic yards (CY): length × width × height in feet, divided by 27.

BEFORE — full of leftovers: Source: tjsclutterremoval.com. What to point at: every room full, floor invisible. Before photos must cover EVERY room — a room with no before photo cannot be billed.

DURING — the cleanout: Source: vangonewyork.com. What to point at: crew, bags, piles leaving. The pile is measured, not guessed.

AFTER — empty, swept, proven: Source: townecountryinspector.com. What to point at: empty room, clean floor, ready for its after photo. The after photo is the proof the job is done.

What is required (typical — always check the order): before photos of every room; debris removed as the order states (never take personal property the order does not cover); honest CY measurement; after photos empty + swept.

How we handle it: order → permission → Vendor Manager dispatches → Field Crew photographs every room before, removes, measures, photographs after → Processor checks the CY math and tags → Client Manager submits.

The CY math (synthetic example, labelled): a pile 9 ft × 6 ft × 3 ft = 162 cubic feet ÷ 27 = 6 CY. If someone bills 15 CY for that pile, the math catches it. Measure honestly — the pile is the proof.

Teams: Field Crew removes + measures. Vendor Manager dispatches. Processor checks the math. Client Manager submits.

Safety: safety before tidy — lift with legs not back, gloves for sharp objects, dust mask where mould or dust is heavy, and never remove personal property the order does not cover.

Cost of getting this wrong: one overstated pile billed at 15 CY instead of 6 CY is a dishonest invoice — rejected, re-measured, and the crew's credibility spent on one job.

SAY: "Debris is where honesty is measured — literally, in cubic yards."

5. Inspection: can you tell the truth about this house?

Task — trainees write the report line: each trainee writes one room in one sentence — no guessing, no adjectives that hide facts.

What it is, plainly: go to the house, look at everything, and write down exactly what you find — occupancy signs, condition, damage. No work, just truth. The report IS the product.

DURING — the inspector at work: Source: bentleyhomeinspection.com. What to point at: eyes open, every corner checked. An inspection that misses a room is a report that lies by omission.

THE REPORT — the "after" of an inspection: Source: inspectcondition.com. What to point at: rooms, systems, damage, written down. The report is what the client pays for.

What is required (typical — always check the order): every room photographed; occupancy signs noted (lived-in signs = STOP + report, do not proceed with work); damage noted room by room; report written the same day.

How we handle it: order → Vendor Manager dispatches → Field Crew inspects + photographs + reports → Processor tags + QCs (blurry, dark, or missing-room photos = send back) → Client Manager submits.

Teams: Field Crew inspects. Vendor Manager dispatches. Processor proves the report. Client Manager submits.

Safety: safety before tidy — never enter an unsafe structure, never climb a failing stair, and if occupancy signs appear mid-inspection, STOP + report; the report is never worth a person.

Cost of getting this wrong: one missed room is a report that lies by omission — the client orders work on wrong facts, and the correction costs a second visit plus trust.

SAY: "An inspection does not fix anything. It tells the truth about everything."

Real workflows — which member handles what, and how hard it really is

This is how the work actually flows in the operation, role by role. Read it as one chain: each role hands to the next, and each handoff is where things break.

Vendor Manager (VM) — the dispatcher

Flow: order arrives → read the scope + the order's rulebook → permission/occupancy check (uncertain = HOLD + escalate, never guess) → pick the crew (right skill, right area, available) → dispatch with scope + lockbox + deadline → confirm the crew accepted → track the visit → collect photos + crew report → check completeness → hand to Processor.

Complexity (this is why the job is hard): many orders at once, crews in different places and time zones, occupancy never certain from paper alone, weather moves deadlines, access fails (wrong lockbox code, locked gates), crews no-show, scopes turn out wrong on site. The VM holds all of that at once and still has to decide: dispatch, hold, or escalate — for every order, every day.

Where it breaks: wrong crew (skill mismatch), missed visit (no confirmation), wrong scope (crew does what the order did not ask), no-show with no backup plan. Every break here becomes the Processor's and Client Manager's problem later.

Processor (Office) — the prover

Flow: field photos + crew report arrive → enter in PPW (the office engine, NOT a client portal) → tag every photo to its line item + proof flags → date-stamp check (required by some clients, not by others — check the order) → quantities measured honestly (CY math, counts) → check allowability against the order's rulebook → build the invoice (allowable → bid → approved) → QC everything (photo clarity, place/time/scope, missing shots) → gaps go BACK to the field, never papered over → prepare up to ready. The owner marks Ready For Office himself — the Processor never marks it.

Complexity: photo volume (dozens per job), tagging accuracy (one wrong tag = one unpaid line), quantity disputes, unallowable items hiding in claims, missing date-stamps, invoice math, different formats per client, rework loops when proof is thin. The Processor is where claims live or die.

Where it breaks: bad photos, wrong quantities, unallowable items billed, missing date-stamps, untagged evidence. A claim built on any of these dies at the client — and the death is discovered weeks later.

Client Manager — the closer

Flow: take the prepared file → submit in the client portal (the client's system — never confuse it with PPW, the office engine) → track review → handle rejections (fix with evidence, resubmit, never argue without proof) → follow-up WOs: a submitted WO is often answered by a DIFFERENT WO at the same property, and paid under the new WO — track the chain, never call the old one "unpaid" until the chain is checked → wait ~60 days for payment (younger dues are lag, not delinquency) → match every payment to its WO → losses: find the reason on the WO's invoice part, beside the payment amounts; property-level adjustments (money received but not spent on a property is adjusted against that property's losses); unexplained losses are driven to zero, never carried.

Complexity: many portals, many rulebooks, rejection reasons that hide in portal corners, the 60-day wait (patience + tracking, not panic), WO renumbering across systems (address is the key, never the number alone), loss reasons that live beside payment amounts and are hard to find, clients who answer slowly. The Client Manager's job is mostly waiting accurately — and hunting precisely when money does not arrive.

Where it breaks: rejected submissions left unfixed, follow-up WOs missed, payments matched to the wrong WO, losses carried without reasons, received-without-pay balances sitting unmatched.

Handoff in one line: VM gets the right crew to the right house with permission → Processor turns field proof into a clean, honest invoice → Client Manager turns the invoice into cash and explains every dollar that did not come back.

Role map + stop-words (say this once, before the money)

Role map: Servicer = the client's loan manager (manages the loan). National = the client's organiser (organises the work across the country). Client Manager = our closer to that organiser (takes our prepared file into the client's system and brings the money back). The chain and the roles are the same journey: Bank → Servicer → National → EPCS (VM → Processor → Client Manager) → Crew.

Stop-words — do not mix them: HOLD = don't start, escalate (permission problem, occupancy uncertain, authority unclear). STOP = stop work already started, report (occupancy signs appear, unsafe structure, system will not hold pressure). HOLD is decided before work; STOP is decided during work. If a trainee asks "HOLD or STOP?", answer with the moment: before work = HOLD, during work = STOP.

Date-stamp decision rule

Rule: read the order first. Date-stamps are REQUIRED when the order's rulebook says so — for example, FHA-insured work under ML 2016-02 requires date-stamped, labelled before/after photos. Date-stamps are NOT required when the order's rulebook does not ask for them. If the order is unclear, date-stamp anyway: a date-stamped photo is never wrong, but a required photo without a date-stamp is unpaid. Always check the order; never guess.

Money — one synthetic invoice in dollars (labelled, say it aloud)

Synthetic invoice (labelled — not a real claim): a job is claimed at $1,000.

  • Claimed $1,000 → Allowable $800. Lost $200, owned by the Processor. Cause: $120 of debris items billed outside the order's rulebook (unallowable — Processor's check missed them) + $80 of work with missing date-stamps and untagged photos (unproven — Processor's proof check missed them).
  • Allowable $800 → Approved $700. Lost $100, owned by the Client Manager. Cause: $100 of the claim was over the allowable limit and was submitted without over-allowable approval — cut at review instead of approved in advance.
  • Approved $700 → Invoiced $700. Lost $0. The invoice matched the approval exactly.
  • Invoiced $700 → Paid $550. Lost $150, owned by the Client Manager with the Processor. Cause: $60 rejection left unfixed too long (Client Manager) + $50 short-paid on a disputed quantity (Processor's measurement + Client Manager's follow-up) + $40 adjusted against the same property's received-but-unspent balance (Client Manager's adjustment, see below).

Every bleed rung has an owner: allowability = Processor; approval = Client Manager; payment = Client Manager with the Processor. Your job is to know which bleed is yours — and stop it before it happens.

Money, continued — short additions (all synthetic, labelled)

Over-allowable process: when a job's cost goes over the allowable limit, approval must come through the claim portal (P260) at least 5 business days before the conveyance due date — conveyance is the formal handover of the house to the insurer. File the over-allowable request with photos and the reason, wait for approval, then do the work. Verify the current schedule and limits before teaching — the 2016 figure is not taught as current.

Gross = net, no client discount: on these orders there is no client discount — gross equals net. A $100 allowable line is a $100 line, not $90. Always check the order; never apply a discount percentage from another client.

One worked follow-up chain example (synthetic, labelled): WO-A at a property is submitted and shows $0 paid. Months later, WO-B — a different number, same property address — is submitted, and $420 is paid under WO-B. Checked by address (never by number alone), WO-A's work was adjusted and paid under WO-B. Never call WO-A "unpaid" until the chain is checked — address is the key.

Loss reason codes (unified list, used in every teaching file): REJECTED (proof rejected — fix with evidence and resubmit); SHORT-PAID (client paid less than invoiced — e.g. quantity disputed); UNALLOWABLE (scope outside the rulebook); ADJUSTED (adjusted against the same property's balance); CHAIN-PAID (paid under a follow-up WO at the same property); UNEXPLAINED (no reason found yet — a queue item, never a verdict; the old UNKNOWN is folded into UNEXPLAINED).

Property-level adjustment, worked numbers (synthetic, labelled): one property received $900 from the client, with $600 of crew cost recorded — $300 received but not spent. The same property has a $250 short-paid loss. Adjust $250 of the $300 against the loss, leaving $50 unspent. Identification: match received vs spent per property by address. Approval: the owner approves the adjustment, the Processor records it, the Client Manager confirms it in the portal.

Unexplained driven to zero — owner and tracking: the Client Manager owns the unexplained queue. Tracking: one line per unexplained item (property address, amount, date, last check). Review weekly until every line has a reason or a chain match. Zero means no line left without a reason — not zero dollars lost.

Decision exercise — decide + defend (PROTECTED 15 minutes)

Synthetic order (labelled): Secure + Grass Cut for your named house. The crew reports: fridge cold, a clock lit, a prescription 4 days old. Approve, hold, or reject? Pairs decide, then write an Update Line of 25 words or fewer. Expert reveal: occupancy is uncertain → HOLD + escalate. No work starts on an uncertain house. Classroom predictions are never graded — reasoning is.

Update Line rubric — 4-point check (all four must pass):

  1. Occupancy signal named? (fridge cold, clock lit, prescription 4 days old — at least one, in words.)
  2. Decision stated? (HOLD, approve, or reject — one word, not hinted.)
  3. Next step named? (escalate to whom, for what check.)
  4. 25 words or fewer? (Count them. Over 25 fails, however good the reasoning.)

Strong example line (18 words, passes all four): "Fridge cold, clock lit, prescription 4 days old: occupancy uncertain. HOLD work, escalate to manager for occupancy check."

Weak example line (fails three checks): "House looks empty so do the secure and grass now, everything seems fine, proceed as normal today." — No signal named, wrong decision, no next step. Reasoning, not the verdict alone, is what the teacher praises or corrects.

Debrief questions (ask both, every time): "What signal changed your mind?" and "What would you do if the fridge were warm?"
