First Class — The Boom: How America Got Houses

TRAINING ONLY — not a real work system. Every case, name, and example on this page is FICTIONAL training material, unless a line is explicitly marked as real industry history. The history figures below are real published figures, flagged wherever sources disagree or a figure must be re-checked before teaching. No client names, no real work data, no real addresses, amounts, or crew names anywhere on this page.

What this class is: the complete first class. Nothing is cut. Teach it in full, at whatever pace the class needs — time is flexible. The teacher decides how deep to go on the day; this page holds everything, so nothing the teacher skips is lost.

How to read this page: SAY lines are scripts — say them as written. Everything else is teacher knowledge — tell it as story, do not read it aloud.

SEAM — if you also teach the today page: teach this page first, then start the today page at work order 1 and SKIP its Beginning section — do not teach history twice. Naming happens once, at entry, on this page.

How to teach it — professor, professional, trainer, in every block: open each beat as professor (concept + why), prove it as professional (numbers + stake + sources), practise it as trainer (name her, answer, decide, say it back). Close each block as professor with one summing line. Check every 10 minutes: one question, one vote, or one line said back — no lecture stretch runs longer than 10 minutes without practice. Plain English always; reasoned wrong answers praised; verdicts judged, never accent, grammar, or speed of speech; no reading bullet points aloud.

Naming beat — once, at entry, before anything else

Method: ask the class to shout three names. Vote by hands. The teacher breaks ties. If there is no agreement in 60 seconds, use the default name "Maple House" (a fictional fallback example) and move on. Write the chosen name on the board. From this moment, say her name aloud wherever this page says "the house", and the class says it back. She is never named a second time. Her name goes on every work order from here on — trainees protect what they named.

Teacher note: without a name, she is a diagram. With a name, she is theirs. Do not cut the naming.

The hook — a baby grows up, a house is born

SAY: "A baby is born in 1952. She grows up, she marries, she starts a family of her own in 1972. And in that same year, 1972, a house is born too. That house is our house. She was built because that baby — and millions like her — grew up and needed a home."

Get this right first, and say it plainly. The babies of the boom were born between 1946 and 1964 — about 76 million of them. By 1972, the oldest of them were grown adults, marrying and starting families. That is why houses were needed in 1972. Never say a baby born in 1972 is a baby-boomer. A baby born in 1972 came after the boom. The boom babies are the ones who grew up and, in 1972, needed a house.

So the story starts with a young family in 1972, standing on an empty piece of land, asking one question: can we have a home of our own? The answer to that question took thirty years to build. This class tells those thirty years, one step at a time.

The boom story — how the house became possible

The war ends. For years, America's factories made tanks and planes and ships. Now the soldiers are coming home, and the factories must make something else — cars, fridges, washing machines, and houses. The word for this is convert: the same factory, the same workers, a new product. But a returning soldier cannot buy a house just because a factory can build one. He needs money, a loan, a road to drive on, and a law that lets him borrow. All of that had to be built too.

First, the world had to be put back together. In 1944, while the war was still going on, leaders from many countries met at a place called Bretton Woods and wrote new rules for world money, so countries could trade with each other again instead of closing their doors. Then, from 1948 to 1952, America sent money to rebuild Europe. That was the Marshall Plan. It sounds far away from one house, but it is not: when Europe could buy and sell again, American factories had work again, and working people had wages again. Wages are the beginning of every house story, because a house is bought with a monthly payment, and a monthly payment is paid from a wage.

Then the soldiers came home. They came home with savings in their pockets — army pay saved up over years — and they found almost no houses to buy. During the war, almost nobody had built houses. That is what a shortage means: many people want a thing, and there are too few of it. So the government passed a law for them. On 22 June 1944, the GI Bill was signed. GI means the ordinary American soldier. The law said: if a veteran wants to buy a house, the government will stand behind his loan. That promise is called a guaranty. It means the bank lends the money, and if the soldier cannot pay, the government covers part of the loss. The guaranty was raised to $4,000 in 1945, and by 1993 the Veterans Affairs department — the VA, the office that looks after veterans — had guaranteed 13.9 million home loans. Thirteen point nine million home loans guaranteed — the government promised the bank: lend to this soldier, we stand behind him.

Return line: so, for our young family — can we have a home? Not yet on a soldier's promise alone — but now an ordinary wage can borrow, and the door is opening.

But a promise to soldiers was not enough. Ordinary families needed loans too, and before the 1930s, home loans were cruel things. A family borrowed for a short time, paid only a little each month, and then at the end one giant final payment came due — a balloon payment, called that because it swells up at the end until it bursts. And to borrow at all, a family had to put down 30 to 50 percent of the price in cash. Almost nobody had that much cash. So on 27 June 1934, the government created the FHA — the Federal Housing Administration. The FHA does not lend money. It insures loans. Insurance means: the bank lends the money, and the FHA promises the bank that if the borrower cannot pay, the insurance covers the loss. With that promise, banks could offer long loans — twenty or thirty years — with a small down payment. The monthly payment became small enough for an ordinary wage. That one change, from short balloon loans to long insured loans, is why ordinary people could start buying houses at all.

SAY: "The FHA does not lend money. It insures loans. That one change is why an ordinary wage could buy a house."

And then came the babies. About 76 million babies were born in America between 1946 and 1964. That is the baby boom. More babies meant more children growing up, more young adults marrying, more new families — and every new family needed a roof. Our house, born in 1972, was built for one of those new families.

Somebody had to actually build the houses, fast and cheap. In Levittown, from 1947 to 1951, a builder named Levitt built more than 17,000 houses the way a factory builds cars: same parts, same steps, one after another, on land outside the city. A house there cost about $6,900 to $8,000. That was a price a working family could reach. The house got simpler, the building got faster, and the suburbs — the rings of new houses around the old cities — began to spread.

Return line: can we have a home? Now there are houses to buy — fast, cheap, and outside the city. The question is no longer whether houses exist, but whether this family can reach one.

But a house far from the city is useless if you cannot get to work. In 1956, America began building the Interstate highways — wide, fast roads joining the whole country. And cars were everywhere now. In 1945 there were 25.6 million cars registered in America. By 1960 there were 61.4 million. The share of households with a car rose from 59 percent in 1950 to 82 percent in 1970. A family could now live in a new suburb, drive to work on a new highway, and come home to a new house. The car, the road, and the suburb grew up together. Take one away and the other two do not work.

More forces, one system — none worked alone

The house was not built by one cause. It was built by many causes pulling together, and the teacher walks through each one slowly, one idea at a time.

Who lent the money? A big part of the answer is a kind of local bank called a thrift, also called a savings and loan, or S&L. A thrift is simple: ordinary people put their savings in, and the thrift lends that money out as home loans. People trusted thrifts with their savings because the government insured their deposits — deposit insurance means that even if the bank fails, the saver's money is safe up to a limit. In the late 1960s, thrifts held about 57 percent of all home mortgages in America. More than half of every home loan came through these local savings banks. That is how big they were.

And then the thrifts broke, and the teacher must tell this part honestly, because it is the first warning in the whole course. Thrifts borrowed short and lent long: they paid interest on savings every day, but their money was locked up in thirty-year home loans. When interest rates shot up in the early 1980s, the thrifts had to pay more for savings than they earned from their old loans, and they began to bleed. Between 1986 and 1995, 1,043 out of 3,234 thrifts failed. The cost of cleaning up the failure is taught as a range, not one number, because honest counts disagree: $123.8 billion of taxpayer money plus $29.1 billion from the industry, against a government auditor's total of $160 billion. In 1989, a law called FIRREA abolished the old deposit insurer and created the Resolution Trust Corporation — the RTC — whose job was to wind the failed thrifts down, sell what they owned, and close them. The lesson for trainees is simple and it will return all course long: a system that looks safe can break when the money underneath it moves.

Zoning is a law that says what can be built where. Single-family zoning says: on this land, only one house for one family may stand — no apartment building, no shop, no flats. The first single-family zoning came in Berkeley in 1916. In 1926, the Supreme Court allowed it in a case called Euclid, and after that it spread fast: from 8 zoned cities in 1916 to 1,246 cities by 1936. In many large cities, roughly three-quarters of residential land is single-family-only — that figure is approximate, and the teacher says so. Zoning shaped America into streets of separate houses, because the law said apartment buildings could not stand there.

Then light and water. A house is not a home until it has electricity, running water, and a toilet that flushes. On American farms, only about 11 percent had electricity in 1935. By 1960, about 97 percent did. Those figures are about farms and rural homes — the teacher labels them as such — because the rural electrification programme wired the countryside. The new suburbs were different: towns and builders laid the wires and pipes as they built the streets. Either way, the point is the same. A house needs wires and pipes before it needs paint.

Then cold air. Air conditioning is a machine that cools a house, and it changed where Americans could live. Before it, the hot South was hard to live in all year. With window units in the 1950s and central air in the 1960s and 1970s, the South and West became comfortable. The Sun Belt — the warm southern and western states — grew from 28 percent of Americans in 1950 to 40 percent in 2000. A machine made a whole region of the country livable, and houses followed the people there.

Then the tax code. The mortgage interest deduction is a rule that lets a homeowner subtract the interest on their home loan from their taxable income, so they pay less tax. Here is the honest part, and the teacher must tell it: this rule was not created to help homeownership. It dates to 1913, when all interest on all loans was deductible — a tax accident, not a housing policy. Later laws kept it for home loans and capped it: a $1 million cap in 1986, cut to $750,000 in 2017. The teacher must verify the current law before teaching, because tax caps change. The rule helped people who already owned, but it was never the reason the suburbs were born.

Then two incomes. For a long time, banks counted only the husband's wage when they decided whether a family could borrow. In 1974, a law called the Equal Credit Opportunity Act said that was wrong: a lender could no longer discount a wife's income. Two paychecks could now buy one house. More families could borrow, and more houses were needed.

Then securitisation, and the teacher must explain this word slowly, because it will matter again when the story breaks. Securitisation means bundling many home loans together and selling pieces of that bundle to investors. The homeowners still pay their monthly payments, but the payments pass through to the investors who bought the bundle. That is why the first bundles were called pass-throughs. The year of the very first one is disputed across sources — Ginnie Mae is dated 1968 in some sources and 1970 in others, and the teacher flags that disagreement rather than guessing. After that, the instrument timeline is clear: Freddie Mac's first pass-through in 1971, Fannie Mae's in 1981, a more complex bundle called a CMO in 1983, and a tax-efficient form called a REMIC in 1986 (CMO and REMIC are plainly ways of bundling many home loans together so investors can buy pieces of the payments). Each step made it easier to turn home loans into investments, and easier money meant more houses.

And the houses themselves got bigger. A typical new house was under 1,000 square feet in 1950. By 1973 it was 1,660 square feet. By 2017 it was 2,631 square feet. Different counts disagree a little, so the teacher teaches this as a trend, not one exact line: houses kept growing as families got richer and land got cheaper at the edge of town.

Behind all of this sat an idea. In 1931, a writer named Adams wrote about the American Dream, and he defined it as opportunity — in his own words, "not a dream of motor cars and high wages merely." A dream of a better life, open to anyone willing to work for it. After the war, the house became the symbol of that dream. A house meant you had arrived. That feeling — that owning a house is what success looks like — pushed millions of families to buy, and pushed builders to build.

The government also promised homes directly. The Housing Act of 1949 declared that every American family deserved "a decent home." It planned 810,000 public homes over six years. It took more than twenty years to build them. Promises about houses are easy to make and slow to keep, and trainees should remember that whenever anyone promises a quick fix.

Building itself changed slowly. Balloon framing — building a house's walls from long, light wooden studs that run the full height of the wall — dates to the 1830s. It made houses cheaper to build than heavy timber frames. But the postwar savings did not come from factory housing. They came from management and components: standard parts, standard steps, crews doing one job well, over and over, like Levittown. The factory idea helped, but the building site, organised like a factory, is what actually cut the cost.

And then the warning. In 1968, a programme called Section 235 let families buy homes with subsidised ownership — the government pushed the interest rate down as low as 1 percent, so families who could barely pay could buy. By the end of 1973, 10 percent of those loans were in foreclosure. Ten in every hundred families lost their homes. The lesson is blunt: pushing ownership without careful lending does not create homeowners. It creates foreclosures. That warning will matter when lending loosens again.

SAY: "No single cause built her. Loans + zoning + funding + highways + cars + utilities + air + tax + culture built her — with a filter."

The filter — prosperity and exclusion, side by side

Now the teacher stops the happy story, because it is only half true. The boom did not lift everyone. Two tools shut Black families and others out of the same boom, and the teacher must teach them in plain words.

Redlining was a map with a red line around it. Banks and government agencies drew maps of cities and coloured some neighbourhoods red, calling them risky. Those neighbourhoods were usually Black neighbourhoods. A red line on a map meant loans were refused there, or offered only on harsh terms. A family could have the same wage, the same savings, the same good character as a family across town — but if their street was redlined, the bank said no. The map decided before the person was ever met.

Restrictive covenants were paper promises written into the deeds of new suburbs. A deed is the paper that says who owns a house. A restrictive covenant in that paper said the house could not be sold to Black families and others. It was written into the house itself, passed from owner to owner, like a rule glued to the front door. Between the red map and the written promise, the boom's houses went mostly to white families, while Black families were pushed to older city neighbourhoods, rented homes, and worse loans.

And the sorting did not stop there. White flight is the name for white families leaving cities for the suburbs, and school and funding sorting is what followed: schools and services were paid for largely from local property taxes, so neighbourhoods with expensive houses got well-funded schools, and neighbourhoods with cheap houses got little. Where a family lived decided what school their children got, and what their street looked like, and years later, which houses fell empty first when trouble came. Prosperity and exclusion must be taught side by side, never one without the other. If trainees only learn the happy half, they will not understand why some streets in this course's later sessions are full of empty houses and others are not.

SAY: "The same thirty years that built her also drew lines around who could live in her. Never tell one half without the other."

Teacher note — do not cut the filter. A boom story without exclusion is a false story.

Two peaks, one loop — never collapse them

PREVIEW — this is the short version. In Class 2 you go deep: you draw the loop yourself, and that is why Class 2 is not a repeat.

Teacher note: this section is a preview — Class 2 goes deep, with trainees drawing the loop themselves, which is why Class 2 is not a repeat.

Keep the two peaks separate, always. Our house is born in 1972, the biggest TOTAL building year — and that word TOTAL matters.

TOTAL housing starts peaked in 1972: 2,379,000 — 1,309,000 single-family plus 1,070,000 multifamily (multifamily means apartment buildings: many families under one roof, not one house for one family). The 1972 peak was multifamily-led: baby boomers forming households, the 1968 Housing Act's 26-million-unit goal, and Section 236 subsidies of about 97,000 units in 1972, at effective rates as low as 1 percent. Then came the 1973 moratorium — a formal halt, the government pausing new subsidised housing programmes — and starts fell to 1,353,000 in 1974.

SINGLE-FAMILY starts peaked in 2005: 1,716,000 (total starts that year: 2,068,000). The 2005 peak was driven by a low average 30-year rate of 5.87 percent in 2005, loosened and subprime lending (subprime means lending to borrowers below prime; prime is the standard for a safe borrower — steady income, good payment history, debts they can carry), securitisation, and speculation — people buying because they believed prices would keep rising.

The trough of 2009 was 554,000 total starts. Starts in 2024 were 1,365,000 total. Read every bar by its label: a TOTAL bar and a SINGLE-FAMILY bar are never the same measure.

SAY: "1972 is total. 2005 is single-family. Never collapse them."

The economy loop, in four steps — teach it as a loop, not a list: jobs and income → ability to pay; interest rates → how much house the same payment buys (peak 18.63 percent in October 1981; record low 2.65 percent in January 2021; 7.79 percent in October 2023); credit standards and government programmes → who can borrow; building → supply, jobs, prices. Then the break: price fall plus payment shock → default. The house did not break. The loop broke. That break is the next class.

Teacher note: do not cut the chart. Trainees remember bars, not numbers read aloud.

Chain map — five links, on the wall for the whole course

One work order travels five links before a single lock is changed. Draw this on the wall now; it stays up for the whole course, and every later lesson hangs on it.

Bank → Servicer → National → Our office → Field crew.

  • Bank: the lender — the institution that lent the money for the house.
  • Servicer: the company that manages the loan day to day — it collects the payments, and when payments stop, it is the servicer who must deal with the house.
  • National: the big preservation company that organises work across all fifty states, because no single company can staff every town in America.
  • Our office: our own team — the team that takes the order, sends the crew, and proves the work. (Company names are not used in teaching material; the link is taught by its job, not its name.)
  • Field crew: the hands on the house — the people who cut the grass, change the locks, and photograph every step.

One order, five links, and if any link fails, the house waits.

SAY: "Bank, servicer, national, our office, field crew. One work order travels all five links before a single lock is changed."

Teacher note: do not cut the chain. Every later lesson hangs on it.

Decision moment — the one force

The teacher turns to the class and asks the session's one decision question. There is no trick in it, and there is no single right answer — the reasoning is what counts.

SAY: "Many forces built her. If you could keep only one — the one force without which she would not have been born in 1972 — which one do you pick? Defend it in one sentence."

Pairs argue for thirty seconds, then a few trainees answer aloud. One says the GI Bill, because without the guaranty the bank would never have lent. One says the FHA, because without long loans no ordinary wage could buy. One says the car and the highway, because without them nobody could live where she stands. One says the baby boom itself, because without new families nobody needed her at all. The teacher praises every reasoned answer, even the ones that disagree, and corrects only guessing and bluffing. The point of the moment is not to crown one force. The point is that trainees now feel the forces as choices, not as a list.

Retrieval, memory anchor, cliffhanger

Retrieval, from memory, no notes. Each trainee says one line: name one force that built her, and one force that excluded. One line each, around the room. The forces are now theirs, because they said them aloud. Do not retell. Let them tell you. If they cannot say it back, it was a lecture, not a lesson.

Memory anchor: the name. They protect what they named. Her name is on every work order from today on. Remember her name, and you will remember why this work matters.

SAY: "They protect what they named. Her name is on every work order from today on."

One last return to the question we started with: can we have a home? She has one now — built by all these forces, and fenced by the filter. Next class, she loses it.

Cliffhanger — the next class: the house lived, and then the payments stopped. What happens to her now? Who steps in? That is the next class — the fall: missed payment, loss mitigation, foreclosure, auction, vacancy, and decay. Do not teach it now. Leave the question hanging.

Teacher notes (not spoken as story)

  • The baby-boom correction is non-negotiable: boom babies were born 1946–1964 and formed households in 1972. Never say a baby born in 1972 is a baby-boomer.
  • The thrift crisis cost is taught as a range ($123.8 billion taxpayer plus $29.1 billion industry, against a $160 billion auditor total), never as one number.
  • The single-family-only land share (roughly three-quarters in many large cities) is approximate — say so.
  • The farm electrification figures (about 11 percent in 1935 to about 97 percent in 1960) are about farms and rural homes; suburban tracts were wired and plumbed separately by towns and builders.
  • The Ginnie Mae first pass-through year differs across sources (1968 versus 1970) — flag it, do not guess.
  • House-size growth is taught as a trend; the counts differ by series.
  • Tax caps ($1 million in 1986, $750,000 in 2017) must be verified against current law before teaching.
  • The chain's fourth link is taught as "our office" by its job, not by company name, per the teaching-material guard below.
  • Nothing in this class uses real work data, client names, real work-order numbers, addresses, amounts, or crew names. All examples are synthetic and labelled.

Guards — never teach these

  • Any client name or identifier, any real work-order number, address, amount, crew name, or payment handle. None appear on this page.
  • Any client rule as universal — the governing rulebook is always the one on the order.
