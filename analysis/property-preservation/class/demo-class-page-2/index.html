Chapter 1 · From memory

No notes. Tell me how she was born.

Before anything new, we go back — from memory alone. Last class, thirty years of forces built one house in 1972. If the class can still say how she got here, they own it. If they can't, today has nothing to stand on.

Memory is the point of this class. A trainee who can say the forces without notes doesn't just remember them — they can use them.

Say her name first

She was named once, in Class 1, and she is never named again. If this is a new group, or the name needs saying out loud to bring her back — here it is. Pick the name the class chose, or type it in.

That's her — the 1972 house. Same house, second chapter.
Say it in class

“No notes. Tell me how she was born. One force that built her, one force that excluded — from memory.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Quick way back in: after the war, America had wages, veterans, and almost no houses. The government made loans safe for banks (FHA insurance, the GI Bill's promise), builders like Levitt built whole streets at once, highways and cars made the suburbs reachable, and millions of boom babies grew up needing homes. That's the happy half. The other half: maps and deed rules quietly kept many Black families out of those new streets. Both halves are true at once — that's Class 1 in one breath.

How to run the recall, step by step

  1. Ask for one force that built her. Take answers cold — no correcting yet.
  2. Ask for the filter: who was kept out, and how?
  3. If a force is missing, don't lecture it back. Ask the question that leads there: “What carried the family to work?” (the car, the highway). “What did the bank need before it would lend?” (the promise behind the loan).
  4. Close it in under five minutes. Today builds on this — it doesn't repeat it.

If you've done this work before

You already know why this matters in the field: every empty house you'll ever open started as somebody's brand-new house, built by exactly these forces. The recall isn't nostalgia — it's the habit of asking “how did this house get here?” before asking “what's wrong with it?” That habit is what separates a processor from a form-filler.

The line to remember: “Built by forces, filtered by rules — she carries both stories in her walls.”

Chapter 2 · The word under every number

A start is a house that has only just begun.

Every year, builders begin new houses. That count is called starts — a start is a house whose building has begun. Count the starts in a year and you know how hard America was building that year.

One word, learned properly now, carries the whole class. Get “start” wrong and every number after it wobbles.

Say it in class

“A start is not a finished house, and it is not a sold house. It is a house that has begun. When I say America started 2.4 million homes in a year, I mean 2.4 million beginnings.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Picture three moments in a house's life. First, the diggers arrive and the foundation goes in — that's the start, the moment the count catches it. Months later the house is finished — a different count, called completions. Later still, a family buys it — a third count, sales. A year can have lots of starts and few finished houses if building slows mid-stream. In this class we work with starts, because starts show intention: how hard the country was trying to build, that year.

How it happened, step by step

  1. A builder breaks ground — the house enters the starts count for that year.
  2. Crews frame, wire, plumb and finish it over the following months.
  3. It enters the completions count when it's done, and the sales count when it's bought.
  4. One house, three different years' statistics, potentially. That's why the label matters more than the number.

If you've done this work before

Same discipline you already use on a work order: a photo date, a completion date, and a submission date are three different facts, and swapping them creates billing chaos. Starts vs completions is the same trap at national scale. Teach the label habit here and it pays off in every later class.

The line to remember: “Read the label before you trust the number.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

The starts figures used throughout this class are the long-running U.S. Census Bureau housing-starts series, as carried in the programme's research — the same series behind the 1972 record in Class 1. Where a figure is total starts or single-family starts, the slide says which, every time.

If this is all new to you

Single-family means one house for one family on its own lot — our house, and the streets around her. Multifamily means buildings with many homes inside — apartment blocks. “Total starts” adds the two together. Keep them apart in your head, because the two great waves in this class are made of different mixes: the 1972 wave was nearly half apartments; the 2005 wave was mostly single houses. Same word — “peak” — two very different events.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Every start is counted in one of the two families when building begins.
  2. Add them and you get the total — the headline number.
  3. But the headline can mislead: a total peak can be apartment-led, while a single-family peak can hide inside a smaller total.
  4. So the rule for this class: never say “the peak” without saying which family — total, or single-family.

If you've done this work before

This is the exact mistake that later shows up in foreclosure numbers — filings quoted as repossessions, combined piles quoted as one pile (Class 3 takes that up in full). The starts split is where the class first learns to catch it. If a trainee says “the peak was 2005” today without adding “single-family,” correct it warmly and on the spot — that reflex is the lesson.

The line to remember: “Two waves, two mixes — never collapse them into one number.”

Chapter 3 · The first wave

1972: the biggest building year America ever had.

Our house was born at the very top of the first wave — 2,379,000 total starts in a single year. Say the word total every time, because the second peak, a generation later, is a different number measuring a different thing.

Nearly half that wave wasn't houses like hers at all. It was apartment buildings — and that detail changes the whole story.

Say it in class

“Our house was born in 1972 — the biggest TOTAL building year. Total. Say the word total every time, because the second peak is a different number.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Imagine every builder in the country breaking ground at once — that's 1972. But look at the mix: for every ten homes begun, roughly four and a half were in apartment buildings, not houses on their own lots. Our house was part of the single-family half. The wave she was born in was bigger than any wave since — but it wasn't only, or even mostly, a wave of houses like hers.

How it happened, step by step

  1. The boom babies (born 1946–1964) reached the age of leaving home and forming households of their own.
  2. Millions of young households needed a first home — and for many, a cheap first home meant an apartment, not a house.
  3. Government aimed the same way: the Housing Act of 1968 set a goal of 26 million new homes in ten years.
  4. Builders responded to both signals at once — houses and apartments, everywhere, at record pace.

If you've done this work before

Hold the contrast for later classes: the 1972 wave filled the country with homes that mostly stayed lived-in; the 2005 wave (Slide 5) is the one whose collapse filled your work queue. Same industry, same word “boom” — opposite endings. The mix and the money underneath decide which ending a wave gets.

The line to remember: “1972 is the total peak — apartments and all.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

2,379,000Total starts, 1972 — the all-time total record
1,309,000Single-family starts within that year
1,070,000Multifamily starts within that year

Figures from the U.S. Census Bureau housing-starts series, as carried in the programme's Class 1 research, where this peak anchors the house's birth year.

If this is all new to you

Subsidised means the government paid part of the cost so the rent could stay low. Section 236 did that for apartment building: it pushed builders' borrowing costs down — effective interest rates as low as 1 percent — so building cheap apartments made financial sense. About 97,000 subsidised units came through it in 1972 alone. Young households needed cheap first homes; this programme is a big reason the buildings existed for them.

How it happened, step by step

  1. The 1968 Housing Act set the national goal and created the tools.
  2. Section 236 lowered the cost of borrowing to build rental housing.
  3. Builders built apartments at a pace the market alone wouldn't have funded.
  4. Young households got first homes; the starts count got its apartment half.

If you've done this work before

The trap to head off, because Class 1 set it: Section 235 was the ownership subsidy for buyers — also with rates down to 1 percent, and 10 percent of its loans in foreclosure by the end of 1973. Section 236 is rental and apartments. Similar numbers, completely different programmes. If a trainee merges them, every fact attached to both turns to mush — keep them apart out loud, every time.

The line to remember: “235 helped people buy. 236 helped builders rent. Never swap them.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

Here's where the 97,000 figure sits, so it never floats free of its year or its programme.

Chapter 4 · A wave can stop

Then Washington turned the tap off.

In 1973 the government paused new subsidised housing programmes — a moratorium, a formal word for a halt. The money stopped, and building fell hard: from 2,379,000 starts in 1972 down to 1,353,000 in 1974.

A wave that took years to build was switched off in a season. Remember that speed — you'll see it again in 2009.

Say it in class

“Cheap money plus young households plus a national goal — that's what a building wave looks like from the inside. And a wave can be switched off as fast as it was switched on.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

A moratorium isn't a slow fade — it's a pause button. The programmes that made cheap building possible were halted, so the projects that depended on them stopped being planned. Starts count beginnings, and beginnings are the first thing to vanish when the money pauses. That's why the fall looks so sudden: the pipeline emptied before the building sites did.

How it happened, step by step

  1. 1972: record building, powered partly by subsidised programmes.
  2. 1973: the government pauses new subsidised housing programmes.
  3. Builders stop planning the projects those programmes funded.
  4. 1974: starts land at 1,353,000 — over a million fewer beginnings in two years.

If you've done this work before

You live downstream of decisions like this. When a client changes a rule or pauses a programme, your order volume moves within weeks — not because houses changed, but because the money and permission around them did. Teach trainees to watch the tap, not just the weather: policy is a volume control for this entire industry.

The line to remember: “Building follows the money — and the money can be paused by a signature.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

2,379,000Total starts, 1972 — the peak
1,353,000Total starts, 1974 — after the halt
−1,026,000Fewer beginnings in just two years

Starts figures from the Census Bureau series as carried in the programme research; the 1973 moratorium on new subsidised programmes is the standard historical record of the period.

If this is all new to you

Statistics describe years; houses live decades. When the national wave fell, nothing dramatic happened on her street — her first family kept cooking, commuting, mowing. A house begun in a boom year doesn't know the boom ended. She would be two years old at the fall, thirty-three at the second peak, thirty-seven at the trough. The waves are the weather of her life, not the life itself.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Born 1972, at the total peak — one of 1,309,000 single-family starts that year.
  2. The wave breaks around her in 1973–74; her family life continues unchanged.
  3. She ages through the quieter decades — systems wearing, owners changing (Class 1 told that part).
  4. The second wave arrives when she is already a mature house. That's next.

If you've done this work before

This is why a property's build year is one of the first things worth knowing on any file: it tells you which wave's standards, materials and systems you're likely walking into — and roughly how old every pipe and roof layer is before you take a single photo.

The line to remember: “Waves pass. Houses stay — and age through every one of them.”

Chapter 5 · The second wave

A different peak. Say which kind, every time.

In 2005, single-family starts hit 1,716,000 — the most single houses ever begun in one year. The total that year was 2,068,000: smaller than 1972's total, because this time the apartment half of the wave was missing.

Anyone who tells you “the peak was 2005” without adding “single-family” has squashed two different waves into one — and lost the story.

Say it in class

“Two peaks, two different numbers: 1972 is the total peak, 2005 is the single-family peak. Any trainee who says ‘the peak was 2005’ without saying single-family has collapsed the two waves — and I'll correct it on the spot, kindly, every time.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Hold the two peaks side by side. 1972: total 2,379,000, of which single-family 1,309,000. 2005: total 2,068,000, of which single-family 1,716,000. The second wave's total was smaller — but its single-family part was the biggest ever. By 2005 our house was thirty-three, watching younger, bigger houses rise on new streets at the edge of town — built by a wave she wasn't part of, but whose ending would decide her fate.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Demand shifted from first apartments to detached houses — the boom babies were now established buyers, trading up.
  2. Money got cheap and lending got loose (next slide takes that apart).
  3. Builders answered with single-family subdivisions, at a record single-family pace.
  4. The total stayed below 1972 because the subsidised apartment engine of the first wave no longer existed.

If you've done this work before

Geography lesson hiding in these numbers: the 2005 wave is why so much preservation work sits in 2000s subdivisions at the edge of town — the newest stock fell hardest when the wave broke. When a file shows a 2004–2007 build in an exurban subdivision, you already know half its biography before the first photo.

The line to remember: “1972 built the most homes. 2005 built the most houses. Different records.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

1,716,000Single-family starts, 2005 — the single-family record
2,068,000Total starts, 2005 — for reference; not a peak
2,379,000Total starts, 1972 — still the total record

Census Bureau housing-starts series, as carried in the programme research. The 2005 total is taught “for reference only” — quoting it as a peak is the exact error this slide exists to prevent.

If this is all new to you

Class 1 left her with a second family who needed two paychecks to keep her — that was the new normal the 1974 Equal Credit Opportunity Act made possible, when lenders finally had to count a wife's income too. By 2005 that family could watch the new wave from their porch: bigger houses, easier loans, prices climbing. Nothing about her own loan had changed. Everything about the market around her had.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Dual incomes became the standard household engine for buying (Class 1, Slide 11).
  2. Cheap rates made each paycheck stretch further (Slide 9 ahead).
  3. Loose lending let neighbours buy who once couldn't — and buy bigger.
  4. Rising prices made every owner, her family included, feel richer on paper.

If you've done this work before

“Felt richer on paper” is the sentence to land. Paper wealth is what later becomes negative equity when prices turn — the family owes more than the house is worth, and the loop's break (Slide 10) stops being abstract. Her family's story in the next class runs exactly through that door.

The line to remember: “A boom feels like winning — right up until it asks for the money back.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

Chapter 6 · Inside the second wave

Four ingredients. One dangerous recipe.

Borrowing was cheap. Lending loosened. Risk was bundled and sold onward. And almost everyone believed prices only go up. Told as one story, those four explain the second wave — and quietly load the collapse.

Confidence is the most fragile building material there is.

Say it in class

“Cheap money, loose lending, bundled risk, and the belief that prices only go up. Say those four slowly — because in a few slides, each one turns around and helps break the loop.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Two plain definitions first. Prime is the standard for a safe borrower: steady income, a good payment history, debts they can actually carry. Subprime means below that standard — lending to borrowers the old checks would have stopped. In 2005 the average rate on a thirty-year loan was 5.87 percent, low enough that an ordinary monthly payment could buy a big house. And the checks themselves were waved through: loans that should have failed the prime test were made anyway, in growing numbers.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Low rates shrank the monthly cost of every loan.
  2. More buyers qualified — and qualified for bigger houses.
  3. Lenders loosened the standards to chase the volume: weaker income checks, riskier borrowers.
  4. Every loosened loan still looked safe while prices rose, because a struggling borrower could always sell at a profit.

If you've done this work before

Keep the moral straight for the class: a subprime loan is a description of the borrower's file, not of the borrower's worth. The failure this slide teaches is institutional — standards were loosened by the people paid to keep them. That distinction matters when trainees later stand in those borrowers' empty kitchens.

The line to remember: “When the checks stop meaning anything, the loans stop meaning anything too.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

5.87%Average 30-year mortgage rate, 2005 — cheap money
1,716,000Single-family starts that same year — the wave it fed

Rate as carried in the programme research alongside the 2005 peak (Class 1, Slide 3 drawer). Prime/subprime definitions are the standard lending vocabulary, defined here as the blueprint teaches them.

If this is all new to you

Class 1 met securitisation: many home loans bundled together and sold to investors, so homeowners' payments flow to the bundle's owners. By the 2000s that machine meant the bank that made a loan no longer carried the risk if it went bad — the risk had been sold onward. Making more loans felt safe. Add the fourth ingredient, speculation — people buying houses not to live in, but because they believed prices would keep rising — and demand fed on itself.

How it happened, step by step

  1. A lender makes a loan and sells it into a bundle within weeks.
  2. The lender's money returns, ready to make the next loan — volume becomes the business.
  3. With risk sold onward, careful checking feels like wasted effort.
  4. Speculators pile in on the belief that prices only rise, pushing prices up — which makes the belief look true, for a while.

If you've done this work before

Notice the thread back to your own desk: when nobody downstream owns the risk, nobody upstream sweats the evidence. That's precisely why preservation runs on proof — photos, dates, verification — the industry rebuilt, in its own workflow, the discipline that lending dropped in this decade. Trainees are learning the repair trade for a failure of checking.

The line to remember: “When risk moves away, care moves with it — unless someone is paid to keep it.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

Securitisation's instrument timeline — Ginnie Mae's first pass-through (dated 1968 or 1970 depending on the source), Freddie Mac 1971, Fannie Mae 1981, CMO 1983, REMIC 1986 — is sourced in Class 1's Slide 12 drawer, including the honest note that the first year is disputed. This slide teaches what that machine did by 2005, not a new set of figures.

An honest note

How much each of the four ingredients “caused” the wave is still argued over by economists. What's documented is that all four were present and feeding each other. Teach the four as a recipe, not as percentages — nobody honest can give you the percentages.

From two million beginnings to barely half a million.

If this is all new to you

In four years the country went from starting more than two million homes a year to starting about a quarter of that. Picture her street's younger suburbs: half-built subdivisions, frames standing open to the weather, builders gone. The trough isn't just a small number — it's the moment the machine that builds houses nearly stops, and the flood of empty ones (Class 1's last slide) begins in earnest.

How it happened, step by step

  1. The second wave's recipe (Slide 6) turned: prices fell, payments shocked, loans failed.
  2. Lenders slammed the checks shut — suddenly almost nobody could borrow.
  3. Buyers vanished; builders stopped beginning houses they couldn't sell.
  4. 2009: 554,000 total starts, against 2,068,000 just four years earlier.

If you've done this work before

Here's the inversion to name for the class: the trough that emptied the building industry filled yours. Preservation volume is counter-cyclical — when starts crash and defaults spike, orders surge. Trainees' jobs exist because of 2009 in the most literal way; the profession's modern scale was born in that trough.

The line to remember: “When building stops, emptying starts — and our work begins.”

Chapter 8 · The machine under everything

A house does not stand by itself. It stands inside a loop.

Jobs and income. Rates. The rules about who can borrow. Building itself. Drawn as a circle — because a line has a beginning and an end, and a loop has neither. Each link feeds the next, and the next feeds back.

While all four links hold, houses stand. This loop is the real subject of the whole course.

Say it in class

“Jobs, rates, rules, building — and back to jobs. That is the loop. While it turns, houses stand. When one link snaps, houses fall.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

First link: jobs and income. A family earns wages, and wages decide whether the family can pay a monthly mortgage at all. No wage, no payment — everything else is detail. Second link: rates. The interest rate decides how much house the same monthly payment can buy. If a family can pay the same amount every month, a low rate buys a big house and a high rate buys a small one, because more of the payment is eaten by interest. Same family, same paycheck — different house, purely because of the rate. Next slide puts real rates on that swing.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Wages arrive → a monthly payment becomes possible.
  2. The rate prices that payment → it buys a lot of house, or a little.
  3. Rates fall → the same payment buys more → more families buy, and buy bigger.
  4. Rates rise → the same payment buys less → buyers shrink or vanish, together, all at once.

If you've done this work before

“Rates are a tide” is the image to give them: a tide lifts every buyer at once and sinks every buyer at once, whatever any individual family did right or wrong. Trainees who grasp that stop blaming families for rate-driven defaults — and start reading a property's story in its dates instead.

The line to remember: “The wage makes the payment possible. The rate decides what it can buy.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

The mechanism here — interest consuming more or less of a fixed payment — is plain loan arithmetic, demonstrated live with real rates on the next slide rather than asserted here. The three anchor rates used in this class (18.63%, 2.65%, 7.79%) are introduced, dated and sourced there.

If this is all new to you

Third link: credit standards and programmes — the rules about who is allowed to borrow. Credit standards are the checks a bank runs: income, debts, past payments. Programmes are the government schemes that help some borrowers — the GI Bill and FHA insurance from Class 1, or the Section 236 subsidies that built the 1972 wave. Tighten the checks and fewer families can borrow, however good their wages; loosen them and more can borrow, however weak. Who can borrow is a decision made by banks and governments, not by families. Fourth link: building itself. When building rises it creates supply — more houses to buy — and jobs: carpenters, drivers, sellers. Those jobs pay wages, which feed straight back into the first link. Around and around, while all four links hold.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Standards and programmes open or close the gate to borrowing.
  2. Families who pass the gate buy → builders build to meet them.
  3. Building employs people → wages rise and spread.
  4. Those wages become other families' payments → the loop turns again, stronger.

If you've done this work before

Now the waves explain themselves: 1972 was links one, three and four surging together (wages, a national programme-push, building). 2005 was links two and three (cheap rates, loosened rules) doing the pushing. Same loop, different links driving — that's the analytical move this whole class is training.

The line to remember: “Jobs, rates, rules, building — and back to jobs. Miss a link and the circle won't close.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

The loop is the programme's teaching model — a way of organising documented forces (wages, rates, lending standards, programmes, construction employment) into the circle they actually form. Each force inside it has been introduced with its own evidence in Class 1 or earlier in this class; the loop itself is the map, not a new statistic.

Chapter 9 · Feeling the swing

The same paycheck. Three different houses.

October 1981: rates peaked at 18.63%. January 2021: a record low of 2.65%. October 2023: 7.79% — in between, but high enough to shrink what a family could afford. Try it below: one monthly payment, three tides.

Rates lift every buyer at once, and sink every buyer at once. No family earns its way out of a tide.

Feel it: what one payment buys at each tide

Set a monthly payment a family can manage, pick a rate, and see the size of loan that payment can carry over 30 years. Same family, same discipline — the tide does the rest.

Principal-and-interest only, 30-year fixed, standard loan arithmetic — taxes, insurance and down payment not included.

Say it in class

“Three anchor numbers, and you must feel the swing: 18.63 in 1981, 2.65 in 2021, 7.79 in 2023. At the first, the same payment bought a fraction of a house. At the second, it bought a palace. The family didn't change. The tide did.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

An interest rate is the price of borrowing, charged every year on what you owe. At 18.63 percent, most of an early monthly payment is swallowed by interest — the loan shrinks painfully slowly, and only a small loan is affordable. At 2.65 percent, interest takes a thin slice, so the same payment carries a far bigger loan. Our house lived through all three tides: her first family borrowed in the high-rate world, her second in the falling one, and today's buyers face the in-between.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Early 1980s: rates climb to the October 1981 peak of 18.63% — buying nearly freezes (and, from Class 1, the thrifts who lent long and borrowed short begin to bleed).
  2. Over the following decades rates trend down, with the 2005 average of 5.87% helping fuel the second wave.
  3. January 2021: the record low, 2.65% — the same payment buys the most house in the story.
  4. October 2023: 7.79% — the tide has gone back out for a new generation of buyers.

If you've done this work before

Dates are diagnostics in your world too: a loan originated at 2.65% behaves differently in distress from one originated at 7.79%, because the payment burden differs before anything goes wrong. When a file crosses your desk, the origination year's rate environment is quiet context for how much slack that family ever had.

The line to remember: “18.63, 2.65, 7.79 — the tide went out, came in, and went out again in one house's lifetime.”

If this is all new to you

A family controls its own wage, roughly — overtime, a second job, a promotion. It does not control the rate. Rates are set by forces far above any kitchen table, and when they move, they move for every borrower in the country in the same month. That's why a rate rise can break thousands of careful families simultaneously: the payment was affordable at the old tide and impossible at the new one, with no mistake made in between.

How it happened, step by step

  1. A family sizes its loan to the rate on the day it borrows.
  2. The tide moves — for adjustable loans, the payment itself moves; for everyone, the value and salability of the house moves.
  3. Refinancing, the usual escape, only works while the tide is low and the family's credit still passes the rules link.
  4. When the tide is high and a job is lost at the same time, two loop links fail together — which is exactly the break, next slide.

If you've done this work before

Hold this against the blame you will hear — from clients, from commentators, sometimes from crew — about families who “bought too much house.” Some did. Many simply borrowed at one tide and were asked to pay at another. The loop teaches structural sympathy, and structural sympathy makes for better files: facts about dates and rates, not judgements about people.

The line to remember: “Judge the loan by the tide it was taken at.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

This drawer makes an interpretive point rather than a numerical one, so its proof is the anchors themselves: a swing from 18.63% to 2.65% within one house's lifetime (previous drawer) is the documented fact; “tide” is simply the honest name for a force that size, moving everyone at once.

Chapter 10 · The break

The house did not break. The loop broke.

Prices fall — a house worth more than its loan is suddenly worth less. At the same time comes a payment shock: a sudden jump in what the family must pay each month. Price fall plus payment shock is the break. The payments stop. That is called default.

Notice what did not break first: the roof did not fall in. The walls did not crack. The money underneath the house broke — and the family fell with it.

Say it in class

“The house did not break. The loop broke. Remember that, and you will understand every empty house you ever walk into.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

A payment shock is any sudden jump between what a family was paying and what it must now pay — or between what it earns and what it owes. It arrives four ways: the rate rises and the payment follows; the wage falls (fewer hours, a pay cut); the job goes entirely; or the loan itself changes — many loose-era loans had easy first years, and when those ran out the real payment arrived like a bill nobody remembered agreeing to. Any one can strain a budget. Landing together with a price fall, they break it.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Prices fall → the house is worth less than the loan; selling no longer rescues the family.
  2. A shock hits the payment or the wage → the monthly maths stops working.
  3. Savings absorb the gap for a while — weeks, sometimes months.
  4. The first payment is missed. Default: the loan is in trouble because payments have missed.

If you've done this work before

Default is a financial state, not a property condition — the house at default usually still has lights on, a family inside, a mowed lawn. Trainees who fuse “default” with “empty house” will misread every status they meet later. Class 3 walks the long road between the two; plant the separation firmly here.

The line to remember: “Default is a missed payment, not an empty house. The road between them is long.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

The four shock routes and the price-fall-plus-shock definition of the break are the programme's researched model of the loop's failure, taught as the blueprint frames it. The foreclosure road that follows default — loss mitigation, the 120-day floor, auction, REO — belongs to Class 3 and is deliberately not taught here; default is as far as this class goes.

If this is all new to you

Stand in front of an empty house and the instinct is to look for physical damage — the broken window, the stripped pipes. This slide reverses the instinct: in this business, houses rarely fall because houses fail. They fall because the money loop that held them failed first; the physical damage comes later, from vacancy itself. Everything in the later classes — the orders, the photos, the invoices — is repair work on houses the loop dropped.

How it happened, step by step

  1. The loop turns → the house is financed, lived in, maintained.
  2. A link snaps → payments stop → default.
  3. The legal road runs (Class 3) → the family leaves.
  4. Vacancy damages the house physically → preservation work begins (later classes).

If you've done this work before

This is the sentence that dignifies the trade, and it's worth letting it land in silence for a beat: preservation workers are not clearing up after careless people; they are the repair crew for a national machine that periodically breaks in the same place. Crews who understand that take better photos and write straighter reports.

The line to remember: “Houses fall because the money loop fails — our work is what happens next.”

The proof — dates, numbers, and where they came from

And she herself is the standing evidence: built 1972, sound through two peaks and every tide since — nothing in her story so far has failed physically. Whatever empties her in the classes to come will arrive through the loop, exactly as this drawer describes. Her biography is the proof of concept.

Chapter 11 · Your turn

Now draw it yourselves.

In pairs, one page between you, no notes: draw the loop — jobs and income, rates, rules, building, a circle with arrows showing how each link feeds the next. Then mark where it breaks for a house like hers, and write one sentence saying why that link, and not another.

Judged on reasoning, never on one “right” mark. A defended wrong answer teaches more than a guessed right one.

Where does your loop break?

Tap the link your pair chose. You'll get the model sentence for that choice — compare its reasoning with yours, then write your own underneath it in your notebook.

Say it in class

“Draw the loop. Now mark where it breaks — and tell me why that link, and not another.”

Open a point for the full notes

If this is all new to you

Nobody is grading your drawing skills. Three things only: (1) the loop is drawn as a circle with the four links in the right order — jobs, rates, rules, building, and an arrow back to jobs; (2) one link carries your break mark; (3) one sentence under it says why that link. If your page has those three, it passes. The sentence matters most — it's where the thinking shows.

How it happened, step by step

  1. Pairs draw from memory — four minutes, no notes, one page between two people.
  2. Pairs mark their break and write their sentence — two minutes.
  3. Swap pages with another pair; check each other against the three-point list above.
  4. Teacher collects two or three sentences aloud — praising the reasoning, correcting only guesses.

If you've done this work before

Every link gets chosen by somebody, and every choice is defensible — that's the design. Rates: the payment jumped past the wage. Jobs: no rate saves a payment with no wage behind it. Rules: the loan should never have passed the checks. Prices: the family owed more than the house, and walked. If the whole room picks one link, the teacher argues another — the argument is the exercise.

The line to remember: “A mark without a because is just a guess with a pen.”

Chapter 12 · The close

The family stops paying. The house is still standing.

She has survived two building waves, three rate tides, and thirty-seven years of weather. Nothing is wrong with her roof. Nothing is wrong with her walls. And yet the most dangerous chapter of her life is about to begin — because the loop underneath her just snapped.

What happens to her now? That is next class — and it is not what you expect.

The logic behind this page

Why Class 2 is built the way it is, why it refuses to repeat Class 1, and what its numbers do — and don't — prove. One teacher to another, before the class walks in.

A sequel, not a repeat

Class 1 previewed the peaks and the loop at story speed — the class heard them inside the house's biography. This page exists because hearing is not keeping. Class 2 spends its whole length on just two ideas — the two peaks and the loop — and makes the trainees practise them: recall the forces cold, separate the peaks under correction, feel the rate swing with their own arithmetic, and draw the loop with their own hands. If the class groans “we did this,” the answer is on Slide 1's second drawer: last class you heard it; this class you prove you kept it.

Why the two peaks are never collapsed

  • Collapsing them teaches a falsehood. “The peak” does not exist. There is a total peak (1972, 2,379,000, nearly half apartments) and a single-family peak (2005, 1,716,000, inside a smaller total of 2,068,000). They differ in size, in mix, in cause, and in consequence.
  • The 2005 total is printed “for reference only” wherever it appears — quoting it as a peak is the single most likely error a trainee will carry out of this class, so the page starves it of every chance to be misread.
  • The same discipline scales. Starts vs completions vs sales, 235 vs 236, and (in Class 3) filings vs repossessions vs combined inventory — one habit, learned here on friendly numbers, applied later where the numbers carry money.

Why the loop is drawn, not shown

  • A shown loop is a poster; a drawn loop is a skill. The decision moment (Slide 11) is the class's centre of gravity — pairs draw from memory, mark a break, and defend it in one sentence. The rubric grades reasoning, never the mark, because every link is defensibly “the” break for some real house.
  • The drawn page becomes the memory anchor — Class 1 anchored on a name the class chose; Class 2 anchors on an artefact the class made. Class 3 opens by redrawing it cold, which is also this page's retrieval check.
  • The calculator is arithmetic, not theatre. It computes a standard 30-year principal-and-interest loan from the trainee's own payment figure, at this class's three documented anchor rates. No stored results, no invented scenarios — the trainee supplies the family; history supplies the tide.

What isn't proven — the open questions, honestly

  • The exact causal weights of the four 2005 ingredients (cheap money, loose lending, bundled risk, speculation) are debated by economists; this page teaches them as a recipe, never as percentages.
  • The loop is a teaching model that organises documented forces — wages, rates, standards, programmes, construction employment — into the circle they form. It is a map, not a measurement.
  • Section 236's “about 97,000 units in 1972” is taught as approximate, because that is how the record supports it.
  • The calculator excludes taxes, insurance, and down payment by design and says so on its face; it demonstrates the rate mechanism, not any family's real budget.
  • Foreclosure law, timelines, and outcomes are not taught from this page — they belong to Class 3, and anything state-specific gets checked against current rules before it is put to a trainee as fact.

Running the class from this page

  • Open cold. Slide 1 is a test before it is a welcome — notes shut, names first, five minutes, then move on. Do not re-teach Class 1; ask the questions that lead back to it.
  • Correct the label, warmly, every time. “The peak” without its family — total or single-family — gets a friendly interruption on the spot, whoever says it, teacher included.
  • Protect the drawing. If time runs short, cut discussion elsewhere — never the draw-and-defend on Slide 11. That page is what the class keeps.
  • This page is the spine, not a guest. If a live Muse segment joins the class, it belongs in the Muse app, projected, in one short mid-class slot — and this page can still carry the whole lesson if the connection ever fails.
  • Nothing here is real client data — the house is a teaching character, and every figure is published history or the class's own arithmetic.
