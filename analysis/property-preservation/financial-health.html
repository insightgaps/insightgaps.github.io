Executive Summary
Question

How much invoiced revenue actually converts to liquid cash, and where does margin erosion occur?

Finding

Less than half of all invoiced revenue is realized as cash ($58.3K collected from $130.7K), driven by rising client validation latencies and a compounding $24.0K in profit leakage.

Impact

Severe working capital compression has forced reliance on revolving credit, resulting in interest and fee charges eating up 15% of the net operating margin.

Action

Prioritize collections against ServiceLink ($25.8K outstanding), audit aging items past 60 days, and adjust pricing/dispatch for low-margin service lines.

Finding 01

Revenue Conversion Failure: Less than half of invoiced value reaches the bank.

Evidence

Out of $130.7K invoiced across 268 work orders, only $58.3K has been received (44.6%). A significant portion remains frozen as outstanding receivables ($45.4K or 34.7%), while $24.0K (18.4%) has been permanently lost to profit leakage.

Operational Implication

The business is experiencing a severe cash flow drag. The collection process is failing to complete its cycle, forcing operations to run on historical reserves or external credit to fund immediate contractor liabilities.

Finding 02

Receivable Concentration Risk: Over half of all outstanding cash is locked by a single client.

Evidence

ServiceLink holds $25,800 of the total $45.4K outstanding receivables. Crucially, 100% of ServiceLink's outstanding balance has aged beyond 90 days, presenting a high concentration of toxic, uncollected cash.

Operational Implication

Single-client concentration makes the operator highly vulnerable. A failure to collect from ServiceLink would immediately wipe out 19.7% of total invoiced revenue, demonstrating that operational volume does not equal liquidity safety.

Finding 03

Cash Recovery Compression: Collections have collapsed while contractor costs remain steady.

Evidence

Monthly cash receipts peaked at $21.1K in July, but subsequently collapsed to under $1K/month from September through December. In contrast, contractor disbursements held steady between $3K and $7K per month.

Operational Implication

This mismatch has exhausted cash reserves. By funding contractor payables out of pocket during low-collection months, the operator has been forced into revolving line-of-credit debt, converting margin into interest expenses.

Additional Dimension

Client-Level Cash Efficiency: Comparing value received vs payouts.

Evidence

MCS generated the most cash ($31.5K received vs $13.3K paid to contractors), while ServiceLink returned only $18.7K received against a higher contractor layout of $19.1K, representing an immediate operational deficit.

Operational Implication

Not all volume is equal. MCS operates at a healthy margin profile, whereas ServiceLink operates at an absolute cash loss before factoring in any business overhead.

Operational Recommendations
  • 01

    Prioritize ServiceLink collection effort: Establish an emergency reconciliation process with ServiceLink to validate and release the $25,800 aged over 90 days.

  • 02

    Escalate 60+ and 90+ day receivables: Freeze further work order execution for clients whose past-due balances exceed established risk limits.

  • 03

    Introduce weekly cash recovery review: Adjust work order intake dynamically based on individual client's payment velocity to prevent reserve depletion.

Methodology

Dataset: Analysis is based on 268 work order records spanning the period May to December 2025. Data source includes operator-provided accounting ledgers and client validation portal logs.

Calculation Logic: Income Ratio is calculated as (Payment Received ÷ Expected Contractor Pay). Leakage represents the margin difference between Expected Contractor Pay and Actual Paid to Contractor.

Limitations: Analysis does not account for internal operational overhead, office staff salaries, or vehicle fuel costs. All figures are anonymized at the operator's request.
