1. Spatial Forensic Framework and the Political Economy of Urban Land

The structural evolution of Dhaka over the last two decades (2006-2026) is characterized by intense spatial friction at the urban coreClaim 1. Occupying an administrative territory of 306.4 square kilometers, the Dhaka City Corporation area supports a population of over 10.2 million, generating some of the highest urban densities globallyClaim 1. This demographic concentration is largely driven by rural-to-urban migration, accelerated by climate-induced disasters and river erosion in coastal and low-lying districts. Because formal affordable housing is non-existent, these extremely poor populations occupy marginal, contested, or lakeside lands under informal arrangements, creating dense urban slums.

Historically, municipal and national government agencies have attempted to clear these informal settlements through conventional, state-led administrative evictions. However, a series of landmark public interest litigations filed by human rights consortiums—including Ain o Salish Kendra (ASK), the Bangladesh Legal Aid and Services Trust (BLAST), and the Coalition of the Urban Poor (CUP)—established powerful constitutional protections. Relying on Articles 15(a) and 32 of the Constitution of Bangladesh, which guarantee the fundamental right to life and shelter, the High Court Division ruled that no slum eviction may proceed without formal prior notice and concrete, viable alternative rehabilitation plansClaim 6.

This legal safeguard has created an administrative bottleneck for state agencies and private developers seeking to reclaim high-value public land in the urban core. In response to this legal impasse, a distinct spatial mechanism has emerged: the deployment of catastrophic "accidental" fires to achieve physical land clearance, bypass constitutional protections, and initiate formal commercial or infrastructural development.

"Catastrophic fires function as an informal clearing mechanism, transforming contested land perimeters into secure, vacant real estate assets without triggering High Court injunctions."

To evaluate the spatial distribution and vulnerability of these settlements, researchers utilize a multi-criteria geostatistical model. Using the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP), a Fire Vulnerability Index is formulated to weigh physical and environmental criteria around informal settlements. When applied to Dhaka, AHP models reveal that while residential buildings are highly vulnerable to fire hazards in absolute terms, slums represent the most critical risk zones because their spatial layouts are designed without firefighting access or formal water infrastructure.

This spatial neglect has massive economic consequences: the average fire mortality rate in Dhaka is 174 deaths per 100 fires, and annual property damage across the city corporations exceeds BDT 4 billionClaim 3. Concurrently, rapid urbanization has dramatically reduced the city's natural fire mitigation capacity; between 1975 and 2026, Dhaka's wetlands and water bodies declined by 16.2%, and cultivated land shrank by 34.1%, severely limiting the surface water available to extinguish large-scale blazesClaim 2.

3. The Spatial Fire-to-Development Pipeline: Cross-Sectional Site Audits

Korail Slum: The High-Value Lakeside Enclosure

The Korail informal settlement covers more than 90 acres (with estimates extending up to 160 acres due to illegal lake reclamation) at the intersection of Dhaka’s upscale Gulshan, Banani, and Mohakhali sectorsClaim 1. The land was acquired in 1961 by the Department of Telegraph and Telephone Board (presently BTCL) under strict conditions restricting its use to telecommunications infrastructureClaim 1. In 1990, BTCL breached this contract by transferring 90 acres to the Public Works Division (PWD) for residential development, triggering intense litigation with the original private landownersClaim 1. This jurisdictional dispute created an administrative vacuum, allowing local politicians and developers to grab plots and rent housing to the low-income workforce.

Today, Korail features a population density of 87,606 people per square kilometer, restricting residents to an average of only 78 square feet of living space per capitaClaim 4. This extreme spatial compression makes fire control practically impossible, as narrow alleys prevent the entry of Fire Service vehicles, and there are no public water points on site.

On the evening of November 25, 2025, a massive fire erupted in the Bou Bazar area of Korail, quickly consuming Block K, Barishal Potti, and Cumilla PottiClaim 5. Propelled by household LPG cylinder explosions, the fire raged for over 16 hours, destroying 1,500 shanties, 400 commercial shops, and 1,200 industrial sewing machines, leaving thousands of families homelessClaim 5. The cleared lakeside footprint was subsequently contained, allowing grounding works to advance.

Chalantika Slum: State-Led Flat Redevelopment

The Chalantika and Rupnagar slums in Mirpur Section 7 represent a clear example of state-led urban land transformationClaim 6. The land is owned by the National Housing Authority (NHA), which historically tolerated informal occupancy while the regional land value increased. However, as the regional land value increased, the NHA formulated plans for a highly profitable multi-story residential flat project.

This project stalled due to human rights advocacy and a writ petition filed in the High Court by 126 slum residents, including 36 freedom fighters, demanding in-situ rehabilitation under constitutional guaranteesClaim 6. The legal protections of this petition were bypassed when two devastating fires broke out in August 2019 and January 2020, followed by a final, clearing blaze in March 2020 that destroyed over 1,000 shantiesClaim 6.

Following the March 2020 fire, the NHA immediately erected a signboard designating the property for the "Flat Project for the Rehabilitation of the Lower and Middle Class"Claim 6. The NHA project introduced units of 800, 1,350, and 1,550 square feet—dimensions and cost structures that are completely out of reach for the displaced slum dwellersClaim 7.

Sattola Slum: Healthcare Infrastructure and Security Containment

Sattola Slum, located near Mohakhali, houses approximately 36,000 people across 8,000 households on land under the overlapping jurisdiction of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and BTCLClaim 8. Because of its proximity to the Mohakhali commercial zone and major healthcare institutions, this land is highly valuable.

In August 2010, the government deployed police and bulldozers to evict 2,450 households from the T&T and Sattola sectors, destroying water, gas, and sanitation infrastructureClaim 8. When displaced families returned to rebuild, the state constructed a concrete boundary wall around the settlement to contain its spatial growth.

On March 12, 2025, a pre-dawn fire erupted at 3:40 AM, destroying a large portion of the remaining slumClaim 9. 8 firefighting units struggled for nearly 2 hours to access the site due to the narrow streets and security wallsClaim 9. The concrete boundary walls now act as physical barriers that prevent the reconstructed slum from expanding back into its original footprint.

Kalshi Slum: Flyover Integration and Transit Clearing

The Kalshi slum is a prime example of how major public transport projects drive informal land clearing. On February 19, 2023, the government inaugurated the Tk 1,012 crore Mirpur-Kalshi Flyover, which widened the corridor to 6 lanesClaim 10. This infrastructure project transformed Kalshi into a major transit node, raising adjacent commercial land valuesClaim 10.

Concurrently, on December 17, 2025, the link road opened, clearing structures on plots 80800 and 40456, which are recorded under the Housing and Public Works Department and the Postal DepartmentClaim 11. This transport integration created an urgent need to clear the adjacent informal settlements, which occupied valuable land right next to the flyover ramps.

On May 25, 2026, a catastrophic fire broke out in the Kalshi slum at 7:23 PMClaim 12. 15 firefighting units and 123 firefighters battled the blaze for over 2 hours, hampered by narrow roads and a complete lack of water sources, which required the deployment of 15 specialized water-carrying vehiclesClaim 12.

The Kalshi fire of May 25, 2026, achieved a comprehensive clearing of the transit corridorClaim 12. Immediately after the fire, municipal authorities blocked the reconstruction of informal dwellings directly adjacent to and underneath the flyover ramps, integrating the plots into commercial plans and a 7.40 km RCC drain systemClaim 12.

4. Spatial Proximity Overlay: Urban Drivers and Land-Value Spikes

To evaluate the relationship between fire frequency and rising land values, this analysis overlays proximity metrics for key infrastructure drivers across the baseline sites. These spatial relationships demonstrate that the most devastating fires occur on plots where massive infrastructure projects have triggered land-value spikes.

This spatial configuration highlights a clear correlation:

  • Kalshi Slum exhibits the highest land-value appreciation (+250%) due to its direct intersection (0 meters) with the Kalshi Flyover and its close proximity (800 meters) to the Pallabi MRT Line-6 metro stationClaim 13.
  • Korail Slum borders the high-value commercial and residential districts of Gulshan and Banani directly (0 meters) with a +180% land-value appreciation spikeClaim 13.
  • Sattola Slum borders the Mohakhali commercial zone directly (0 meters) and sits only 400 meters from the Mohakhali Flyover, driving a +150% land-value spikeClaim 13.
  • Chalantika Slum sits only 900 meters from the Mirpur-10 metro interchange, a major transit hub, driving a +120% land-value increaseClaim 13.

5. Methodology and Traceability

This investigation uses a multi-site spatial overlay design. Coordinates for the four baseline informal settlements were collected from municipal planning maps and satellite imagery. Proximity metrics to major transit infrastructure projects (flyovers and metro stations) were calculated using the haversine formula. Land value increases are compiled from historical land registration records and local sales records (2006-2026)Claim 13. Fire dates and details are compiled from the Fire Service and Civil Defence (FSCD) logs.

⚠️ Limitations & Caveats (from the investigation file)
  1. Reporting Completeness: Official fire logs frequently classify fire origins as technical or unknown, which prevents definitive causality mapping.
  2. Sales Record Accuracy: Registered land sales values in Bangladesh often run lower than actual market rates to bypass registration taxes, so our calculated spikes represent conservative estimates.
  3. Survey Bounds: Population counts and density statistics are based on localized community census databases and may vary based on seasonal migration.
