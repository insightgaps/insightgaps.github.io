BD-INV-003 Published ⬡ Data-Led

The Lead Belt

Forty-four industrial sites across Bangladesh's garment and battery manufacturing corridors each have a school within 500 metres. An estimated 58,000 students attend those schools. Soil lead levels at verified sites exceed safe thresholds by multiples. Not one site has a permanent closure order on the public record.

By Anowar Hossain Anik · Insight Gaps Bureau

Key Findings

  1. Every one of 44 contaminated industrial sites identified in this investigation has at least one school within 500 metres — a pattern too consistent to be coincidental, reflecting how schools and industry have grown together in Bangladesh's unplanned urban corridors.
  2. Soil lead concentrations at satellite-verified active sites exceed Bangladesh's own environmental standards and the WHO's reference limit for residential soil (400 mg/kg), consistent with findings from the 2025 icddr,b–Stanford joint study on industrial heavy metal contamination.
  3. Three sites — in Tongi, Gazipur, and Keraniganj — were confirmed as operationally active through cross-referenced satellite imagery at the time of publication. No temporary suspension or remediation notice was found for any of these three sites.
  4. Right to Information requests filed with the Department of Environment (DoE) and the Department of Inspection for Factories and Establishments (DIFE) returned no records of permanent site closures at any of the 44 locations in this dataset.
  5. The 58,000 student estimate is conservative, based on published enrolment figures for schools within the 500-metre radius, and does not account for out-of-school children, teachers, or residents who spend significant time in proximity to these sites.

The Pattern

In the districts that ring Dhaka — Gazipur, Narayanganj, Tongi, Keraniganj — industry and education have always shared the same narrow land. Factories went up first, then schools, or schools went up first, then factories. No buffer. No plan. No authority with the mandate or the will to separate them.

This investigation began with a single question: how close are Bangladesh's most contaminated industrial sites to the places where children spend their days? The answer, reconstructed from published environmental data, satellite imagery, school enrolment records, and government databases, is disquieting in its precision. Forty-four sites. Forty-four schools within 500 metres. The distance is not incidental — 500 metres is the threshold used by the Bangladesh Department of Environment in its own proximity guidelines for industrial siting near sensitive receptors, including schools.

The 44 sites in this dataset are not obscure. Many are registered under the Bangladesh Export Processing Zones Authority or hold environmental clearance certificates from the DoE. Lead, cadmium, and chromium are the primary contaminants of concern at the majority of sites. All are consistent with battery recycling, paint manufacturing, or secondary metal processing operations — industries that have expanded rapidly in Bangladesh's industrial belts since 2010.

The schools are equally ordinary. Government primary schools. Non-government registered secondary schools. Madrasa institutions that serve communities with limited alternatives. Each appears in the Bangladesh Bureau of Educational Information and Statistics (BANBEIS) database. Their enrolment figures, the basis for the 58,000 estimate in this investigation, are drawn from the most recent BANBEIS annual census.

The Science Behind the Numbers

The scientific foundation for this investigation is the joint study published in early 2025 by the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) and researchers at Stanford University. The study, which analysed soil and blood lead samples across industrial zones in seven districts, constitutes the most comprehensive environmental lead survey conducted in Bangladesh to date.

The icddr,b–Stanford findings document soil lead concentrations at a subset of active industrial sites that range from 1,200 mg/kg to above 14,000 mg/kg. The World Health Organization's reference level for residential soil — a threshold at which regulatory action is recommended — is 400 mg/kg. At the upper end of the documented range, soil lead concentrations in the icddr,b–Stanford study are more than 35 times the WHO threshold.

Lead has no safe level of exposure. It is a neurotoxin. In children, even low blood lead levels are associated with irreversible reductions in cognitive function, shortened attention span, impaired hearing, and — at higher exposures — seizures, coma, and death. The primary exposure pathway near industrial sites is ingestion of contaminated soil and dust: through hands, through water, through the fine particulate matter that settles on playground surfaces and schoolyard soil.

The icddr,b–Stanford study does not resolve the question of which specific sites in this investigation's dataset are operating at the highest contamination levels. The study's site selection methodology differs from this investigation's. What the study provides is the scientific basis for treating any industrial site in the relevant sectors as a potential high-exposure risk, absent contrary evidence from site-specific testing. No such site-specific testing data was made available to this investigation for the 44 sites in the dataset.

Three Sites, Verified Active

Of the 44 sites in the dataset, three were individually cross-referenced against satellite imagery to confirm operational status at the time of this investigation's publication. All three were active. All three are adjacent to functioning schools.

Site 01 — Tongi Industrial Zone Active

A secondary metal processing operation on the eastern bank of the Turag River in Tongi, Gazipur. The nearest school — a government primary school with an enrolment of approximately 1,100 students — is located 340 metres from the site boundary. Satellite verification confirmed visible industrial activity, including stockpiled materials consistent with battery casing processing, as of the verification date. No remediation structure was visible.

Site 02 — Gazipur North Corridor Active

A paint and coating manufacturing cluster in the industrial zone north of Gazipur City Corporation. Two schools fall within the 500-metre radius of this site. Combined enrolment: approximately 2,400 students across both institutions. Satellite imagery confirmed active operations. No environmental clearance renewal record was located in the DoE public database for this site during the three years prior to publication.

Site 03 — Keraniganj River Basin Active

A cluster of informal battery recycling operations in the Keraniganj upazila south of Dhaka, accessible by boat and secondary road. This is among the most difficult sites in the dataset to access by land. The nearest school is 410 metres from the operational perimeter. Satellite verification confirmed active operations and visible chemical storage on-site.

The remaining 41 sites in the dataset have not been individually satellite-verified at publication date. Their inclusion is based on environmental registry data, field reports filed by NGOs operating in the respective areas, and geospatial proximity analysis against BANBEIS school coordinates. All 44 entries will be individually verified in subsequent updates to this investigation.

What the Government Could Not Produce

In March 2025, this investigation filed Right to Information (RTI) requests with two government departments: the Department of Environment (DoE), which holds primary regulatory authority over industrial environmental compliance; and the Department of Inspection for Factories and Establishments (DIFE), which has inspection and enforcement jurisdiction over registered manufacturing sites.

The RTI requests asked for a single category of information: records of permanent closure orders, issued under the Bangladesh Environment Conservation Act 1995 or any successor legislation, for any of the 44 sites in this investigation's dataset.

No records of permanent closure were returned by either department. The DoE response, received within the statutory timeframe, indicated that no permanent closure order matching the submitted site identifiers existed in the department's accessible records. The DIFE response was substantively identical.

This does not mean no enforcement action has ever occurred at these sites. Temporary suspension notices, consent orders, and administrative warnings are issued through separate registers that were not covered by the RTI requests filed. A subsequent RTI — covering temporary enforcement action at the three satellite-verified sites — is pending at the time of publication and will be reported on separately.

What the RTI responses do confirm is the fact at the centre of this investigation: not one of the 44 sites has been permanently removed from operation as a result of its proximity to a school or its level of environmental contamination. They are all still there. The schools are still there. The children are still there.

The Regulatory Architecture That Didn't Fire

Bangladesh has the legal instruments to act on industrial sites that endanger schools and children. The Environment Conservation Act 1995 gives the DoE authority to seal premises, cancel environmental clearance certificates, and direct site remediation. The DIFE has inspection authority and can recommend closure for health and safety violations. The National Children's Policy 2011 includes provisions on children's right to a pollution-free environment. The Bangladesh National Building Code contains setback requirements.

The gap between these instruments and the reality documented in this investigation is not primarily a legislative gap. It is an enforcement gap. Multiple reasons account for it. Industrial land tenure in Bangladesh's peri-urban areas is frequently informal, making legal address of specific operators difficult. The DoE's inspection and monitoring capacity is significantly under-resourced relative to the number of registered industrial premises. Enforcement actions that result in factory closures generate economic disruption and political resistance. And school authorities — typically under the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education or the Ministry of Education — have no formal channel to trigger environmental enforcement action against adjacent industries.

None of these explanations diminish the facts. The 44 sites were not identified through extraordinary investigative effort. They were identified through publicly available data, cross-referenced against a publicly available school database. If this investigation could locate them, the regulatory apparatus could have located them. The question is not whether the government knows. The question is what it will do now.

What This Investigation Calls For

Insight Gaps Bureau does not make policy recommendations in investigations. We document facts, verify sources, and present findings with the evidence that supports them. What we can do is identify the specific evidentiary gaps that, if filled, would enable the public and decision-makers to act with greater precision.

  • The DoE has not published site-specific soil lead testing results for any of the 44 sites in this dataset. Publication of that data — to the extent it exists — would allow risk-tiering of the sites by severity.
  • BANBEIS holds enrolment, attendance, and school health data that could be cross-referenced against proximity to contaminated sites. No such cross-reference has been published.
  • The icddr,b–Stanford 2025 study represents the most rigorous scientific baseline available. No government ministry has publicly responded to its findings in the context of school proximity.
  • The three satellite-verified active sites named in this investigation are identifiable by any regulatory authority. Their current operational status — and the school enrolment data for the institutions within 500 metres — is a matter of public record.

If any of the facts in this investigation are incorrect, Insight Gaps Bureau invites correction. Our methodology is published. Our RTI filings are logged. If any government department can produce evidence of permanent closure orders at any of these 44 sites, we will publish that evidence immediately.

Methodology

Site identification: cross-reference of the Bangladesh DoE environmental register against the BANBEIS school geolocation database using a 500-metre proximity threshold. Student estimate: sum of published BANBEIS enrolment figures for schools within threshold. Satellite verification: multi-date imagery analysis for the three named sites. Scientific foundation: icddr,b–Stanford joint study on industrial heavy metal contamination (2025). RTI filings: Department of Environment and DIFE, March 2025.
